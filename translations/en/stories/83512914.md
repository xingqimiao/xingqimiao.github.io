This is an account of how I recognized myself. There are no splendid plot turns, and no clear narrative of "knowing since childhood". It is more like a long, silent trek — instinctual but of unknown purpose — until one night, all the shards came together.

I will tell it in chronological order, from the beginning.

## I. Childhood: The Feelings Without Names

As far back as I can remember, the bathroom was a very special place.

After showering, drying off, in that brief interval before getting dressed, standing alone facing my naked body, I would be seized by a powerful shame and unease. It wasn't the feeling of "someone might see me" — no one was watching; it came purely from my own body itself, radiating outward from my chest and genitals. It was an overwhelmingly negative experience, impossible to dodge, utterly beyond my control. I could only stand there waiting for it to pass. Sometimes getting dressed relieved it, but more often it lasted a long while, spreading all the way to bedtime.

I thought everyone felt this way, so I never mentioned it to anyone.

But that unease did not only appear in the bathroom. It was a pervasive ground tone — it could descend without warning on the bus, during morning reading, or deep in the night. The bathroom version was acute; the everyday version was quiet and persistent, like background noise.

My whole childhood was steeped in that dark undertone. There were, of course, warm and happy moments — but they were like scattered flecks of light floating over a dark river whose name I could not speak. I once blamed my parents for not giving me a happy childhood; I long believed they had done many things wrong, and once said in anger: "My childhood was not happy — you failed to give me a happy one." It is true that my childhood was not happy, but fundamentally, it was not their fault.

There was another thing I was terrified of from childhood: ghosts. Not because of horror films, nor rumors — I was simply afraid of "ghost" as a concept. Rationally I knew ghosts did not exist, but at night that fear bypassed reason entirely and controlled me completely. Sometimes I would get up to read, or sleep with the lights on; sometimes I called my mother to stay with me. It wasn't until the eighth grade that I finally stopped asking.

Looking back now, that "ghost" was very likely a vessel I built for that nameless, inexplicable existential anxiety. I projected my fear of my own body and existence onto ghosts, so that at least I could fear something that "traditionally deserved fear". Later, as I moved closer to my true self, the ghost gradually disappeared. Thinking back, it occupied nearly my entire childhood and caused severe sleep deprivation — yet I never once asked myself why. To some extent my parents were at fault: facing such abnormal ghost-fear, they never thought to have me looked at. Of course, they can't be entirely blamed either — it's affected by views, education, and a lot of other things.

I also didn't like bathing. For a long time I thought it was because my parents told me to shower less to save water — until the second semester of senior year, when I hesitated nearly twenty minutes before a shower and finally realized that the force stopping me came from inside. Later, my father confirmed it: "You've never liked washing since you were little; it takes a call to get you in." I can now clearly distinguish — I don't dislike the sensation of hot water running over me; that feels fine. What I dislike is the moment of facing my body after the shower. My subconscious had long begun avoiding this for me, before I knew the truth.

Around grades 3–4, my chest started hurting. My parents took me to the hospital, and the doctor ordered a six-hormone blood panel — but they didn't let me do it, saying it would pass. I really wanted the test, really, really badly — but I dared not say so, and went home unwillingly. The matter did pass later, and by all rights it should have been archived in my memory as an ordinary "illness" — yet it stayed in my mind, never forgotten. I believe the body remembered it: it had briefly deviated from the "typical" track.

These feelings, I never mentioned to anyone. Because even I didn't know what they were.

Only now do I understand — my body and my mind had been telling me, before I could even speak, that something was wrong.

## II. Middle School: The Golden Age of the False Self

The three years of middle school were a strange "blank window".

If you asked the people around me about that me, they'd probably describe a sunny, clever, high-achieving boy. I myself thought those years were going well: I loved quantum physics and astrophysics, had good friends, studied efficiently, grades climbed steadily.

But this was not healing; it was my brain finding a cleverer way to cope with that nameless pressure. I poured all my psychic energy into the role of "student", immersing myself in the acquisition of knowledge and the rewards of society. I had no spare capacity to feel my body — or rather, I actively severed the connection to it. This was a textbook "false self" — a fully functional self built to adapt to, and win approval from, the environment. It operated so successfully that even I believed my childhood pain was over.

But the repression did not vanish; it leaked out in another form.

From middle school on, I fell into compulsive, excessive masturbation — at a frequency that frightened and disgusted me. After each time, there was no relief, only emptiness and shame. I tried to restrain myself and always failed. Now I understand: it was not simply desire; it was anxiety, locked away by the false self, searching for an outlet.

Meanwhile, the environment was also quietly disciplining me.

I remember in the lower grades of primary school I instinctively wore my watch on the right wrist. Someone told me, "Boys wear it on the left; only girls on the right", so I switched to the left — and kept it there to this day. I am left-handed by nature: when playing cards I hold the deck in my right hand and sort with my left; as a child I liked drawing with my left hand, until I was corrected into a "right-handed person".

I also liked things marked as "belonging to girls": wind chimes, folding fans, doodling on the little blackboard, and later, women's perfume I secretly carried in my schoolbag to smell. Every time, most of my parents would say: "What's a boy playing with that for?" My mother also mumbled about "manly spirit" frequently — I always resented it, but I lowered my head and let it pass.

Starting in seventh grade, I taught myself opera singing — I was very good at it, but never dared sing at home, only when alone, or at school. Because I knew it didn't fit the gender performance expected of me.

Individually, these details look trivial. But they were like tiny cracks hidden beneath three years of a "perfect" false self. Outside, I was an outstanding boy; in the corner of my heart, something seemed inexplicably off.

For three years of middle school, I lived as the excellent, normal boy everyone expected. But I know that was not the real me — just a stage I built with care, without even noticing.

## III. High School: Collapse of the False Self and Nihilism

From high school on, everything began to go wrong.

I sank deep into nihilism. From the first year, I had no interest in the future, long submerged in an indefinable depression. I didn't know where the low feeling came from; I only felt my thinking idling, endlessly pondering the meaning of existence and the meaning of meaning — while reason told me this was meaningless self-consumption, yet I couldn't stop. Suicidal ideation became the background sound of my high school years. I kept rationalizing suicide, trying to prove it was not an impulsive choice but a logically correct option. But I could never get past one fundamental hole: I could not prove that "now" was the moment to execute. That tormented me, yet also served as a constant brake.

My grades fell; I lost interest in studying. And my attribution was the same as the unhappiness of childhood: my brain needed an external reason to explain internal pain, so I first blamed my parents' unfulfilled promise of a hundred-thousand-yuan scholarship, then various external factors. In truth, none of those were the real root.

In the first year of high school, I encountered the concept of the transgender community. I already felt an unspeakable emotion toward MtF people, like a deep resonance. They made me feel... tenderness? Gentleness? Kindness? Excitement?... I can't pin it down. If forced to say: I think every MtF, after going through physical and mental hardship and pain beyond what ordinary people experience, is still a kind and brave soul who treats this world gently. But back then I didn't connect the concept with myself.

First, at sixteen, I identified as non-binary — neither fully a boy nor fully a girl. Now I see that as the transition zone I built for myself: a buffer space before the real identity arrived.

At the same time, I was exploring my sexual orientation. In primary and middle school I had liked a few boys — genuinely, from the heart, though I never expressed it. In ninth grade I fell for a girl for the first time; she rejected me. Later I inferred I was pansexual with a male preference.

My method of exploring orientation was: real feelings in childhood; no concept at the time; retrospective naming later. That is exactly the method I used for gender identity — feeling before language, experience before concept.

There's another thing I now somewhat regret.

In the summer between first and second year of high school, I voluntarily requested circumcision. My stated reason: "the foreskin scratches against hair, it's uncomfortable." The post-op instructions mentioned one could take Mercilon to suppress erections, so I had my mother buy a box. But deep inside, the purpose of that medicine was not suppressing erections — it was an indescribable psychological comfort. I took about ten pills in total.

And the deeper, more hidden layer of motivation: a few years earlier, a relative who worked as an ER doctor had told me that after this surgery one has to wear a skirt for two weeks. I had long yearned for that moment — because it was the only legal way for me to wear a skirt at home. During those two weeks in a skirt, my mood was genuinely good — even though it was just a flimsy house dress. I photographed my skirt hems in the mirror with my phone.

I wasn't "recovering from surgery"; I was using a legal gap created by surgery to breathe, secretly. My subconscious, in order to satisfy that suppressed core longing, wielded a perfectly socially acceptable reason to manipulate my conscious mind into an extremely transgressive act of self-gratification. Which actually proves an important principle: when I am given a legitimate "identity shell" that does not challenge the existing order, my true self can surface safely.

I spent three years of high school grinding through that mire of nihilism, confusion, and secret exploration. On the surface I still attended class, took exams, joked with classmates. Inside, I no longer knew what I was doing. I cultivated a huge number of hobbies, trying to make myself feel fulfilled — but really, my brain was protecting me, keeping the final line from breaking.

Only now do I understand: that nihilism appeared because I had always lived inside others' expectations, inside the false self I'd built. I never knew what the real me actually wanted. Not the countless hobbies, not the answer to "the meaning of meaning" — but the real me itself.

## IV. Epiphany: All the Shards Connected

It was 9:30 PM on March 31, 2026.

I was drifting through the evening study hall, as I had countless times before, imagining myself in women's clothes. Then, suddenly, without warning, the memories of childhood shame surged up in an instant. It was as if some circuit in my brain, blocked for years, suddenly came alive: the shards of childhood — the shame, avoiding the bath, the ghost-fear nights, the chest pain, my parents' disciplining, the loss of the wind chimes and folding fans, the opera singing I dared not do, the fantasies of women's clothes — they all connected at once.

It wasn't deduced by logic; nobody told me. It was a cleansing that struck straight to the soul. For a long time I could not settle. Like an exile suddenly receiving word of recall from the capital. Like a person who always believed himself deaf, suddenly discovering his ears were merely stopped up.

But I didn't stop there. Next, like a detective, I went back through every trace of my seventeen years, questioning and verifying again and again.

**Layer one, comparison with others.**

I asked my peers of the same age — no one had my kind of causeless shame about their childhood bodies. But an MtF friend said he had similar experiences.

**Layer two, my father's testimony.**

He confirmed in person that I had disliked bathing since childhood — overturning my old misunderstanding that "my parents wouldn't let me".

**Layer three, parallel-pattern comparison.**

My way of exploring orientation and my way of exploring gender identity were highly consistent — in both, childhood feelings preceded concept, naming came later. If gender identity were something I fabricated, then would orientation be fabricated too? Obviously not.

**Layer four, true tendency in a drunken state.**

The next night after the epiphany, I drank; near intoxication, I still yearned for that picture of wearing women's clothes, free and unbound. A cisgender person with their guard down wouldn't repeatedly fantasize about becoming another gender.

**Layer five, physical and behavioral change after the epiphany.**

This was the most shocking part to me — because those changes were not under my control; they happened spontaneously.

The dizziness and headaches that had plagued me for over a decade with no organic cause found disappeared almost completely within days of the epiphany. (That headache had accompanied me nearly my whole life: onset in primary school, usually in the afternoon, located below the crown, impossible to relieve; sleeping might help, or more likely left me in more pain afterward. In high school it evolved into post-lunch and post-dinner dizziness. I was finally able to eat meals in peace at school; on weekends I no longer needed caffeine tablets to avoid dizziness.) This is an objective fact that cannot be fabricated.

At the same time, my once-regular masturbation (once every one or two days) dropped sharply, followed by a strong, genitally-directed sense of shame after each occurrence. Not the regret after hedonism — but the fact that I could not accept who getting pleasure that way. All this happened without any medication intervention or subjective intention of abstaining.

I had been drinking regularly since the first year of high school — enough for a moderate alcohol use disorder. After the epiphany I spontaneously found alcohol "taste bad" and naturally stopped. Not because I wanted to quit drinking, but because my body no longer needed it.

These changes were neither a product of my imagination nor deliberately controllable. If I were "fabricating" a transgender narrative, I could not have premeditated or performed the disappearance of a headache; if it were coincidence, it cannot explain the synchronized changes in libido and alcohol consumption.

**Layer six, childhood masturbation patterns.**

This may not count as strong verification, but I want to write it down anyway. My masturbation pattern from grades 3–4 was already different from the typical male pattern: I rejected erection and obtained pleasure in a way closer to a female sexual response pattern. That was an internal script I had before ever seeing any adult film.

**Layer seven, the military training episode.**

The military training after middle school graduation was the environment closest to forced gender-binary division of my life. From the first day I was in low spirits, feeling a pane of glass between me and the world; then came emotional blankness and thought blankness; behavior became performance and compliance. That is the earliest traceable origin of my depression and dissociation.

**Layer eight, ruling out other explanations.**

If this were normal adolescent fluctuation, my shame began in early childhood; if this were influence from the internet, I had these feelings before I ever encountered the concept of transgender; if this were escape from college-exam pressure, then after the epiphany my somatic symptoms improved while anxiety did not disappear — it merely changed form. Ruled out one by one, every arrow points the same direction. By that point I had sufficient reason to take this identity seriously.

Finally, a small detail I have never told anyone. Once during a New Oriental English class, I had a crush on a boy in the class, and my deskmate saw through me: he called me "gay". But at the time I didn't think I was "gay" — because in my heart, liking him was perfectly natural.

Even though I hadn't realized it yet — when a girl likes a boy, how could she think she's done something wrong?

After all these verifications, I found no evidence capable of fully overturning the hypothesis "I am a transgender woman".

This was not something I "thought up". It was something I "discovered". Like a geologist finding a fossil: he didn't create it; he recognized it.

My story differs from many others. I didn't cry "I'm a girl" at three; I didn't only play with dolls; I didn't have a resolve firm enough to die for it from childhood. My road was silent, hidden, encapsulated. I spent over a decade pressing that pain down, and these two years digging it out, piece by piece. I passed through non-binary, through countless nights of self-doubt, through the anxiety after identity was established — only then could I finally say:

I am a transgender woman. MtF.

This is not what I chose. It is what I discovered.

## V. Reflection: Not a Fairytale, but the Only Road

My first reaction after the epiphany was not "release" or "longing" — it was rage.

God-fucking-damn it.

Why me? Why did I, since childhood, have to bear gender-dysphoria attacks that others will never face in their entire lives? Why did I have to endure headaches with no findable cause for so many years? Why did my seventeen years of life amount to not being my true self?

Fifty-fifty odds of being born male or female — damn it, this is the biggest joke God ever played on me.

I did not choose this; nobody influenced me into this; it is fate playing tricks: giving my female soul a male body. If I could choose, I would rather have been cisgender from the start — either male or female, so long as life could be easier. But that is not mine to choose. It is not my fault, nor anyone's — it is my reality.

Heaven dealt me a bad hand, and it took me seventeen years to read my cards. But after reading them, I refuse to accept it. I mourn the nameless pain of my childhood; I sigh at my fate. But after the grief, I must stand up. I will grab these cards and throw them back in his face, and live as that true self — the self whose soul and body are unified.

One of my MtF friends once wrote: "Tragedy is always great — but it belongs on a stage for people to watch; it is not worth personally walking to the end of the world for."

That is my understanding of fate.

## VI. The Road After: Collapse, Struggle, Still Moving Forward

On May 2, 2026, I came out to my parents in a letter. The next day a fierce conflict erupted: after my mother said "be a real man", I hyperventilated, collapsed, and then entered a dissociative state.

Afterwards I went through many psychiatric visits, hospitalization, and medication adjustments. There were several panic attacks, aborted-suicide attempts, self-harm, and medication abuse. The days around the college entrance exam were extremely chaotic: I bounced between hospitals, school, and home.

In the end I still took the gaokao: 605 points. Without all this, the 0 and 5 might have been reversed.

About these negative experiences, I don't want to recount each low point here — but I must admit they happened. Because they too are part of this road.

Across all psychiatric visits up to August 13, the diagnoses I received include: anxiety state; gender identity disorder?; generalized anxiety disorder; panic disorder; anxious-depressive state; sleep disorder. I believe additional diagnoses should include complex PTSD, non-suicidal self-injury disorder, and alcohol use disorder.

During summer vacation, I ordered HRT medication online and began HRT on my own on June 29. During this period came an acute depressive episode (stress from my mother), and fearing hormone fluctuations, I paused the medication on July 14 — 16 days in total. From July 23, nipple tenderness began, and development followed. Once the body's development starts, it's very hard to persuade yourself to stop. So on July 27 I resumed. On August 9, thanks to a relative's connection, I had my sex hormones tested at a hospital — already in the target range. Consider it my 18th-birthday gift.

**Between the night of July 21 and the early morning of July 22, I wrote an essay called "Tense Suspension: On a Neglected Absurdity". It is a philosophical reflection on the rift between life, logic, and time — and a person who has struggled for years trying to find a road to keep walking. You can find it on Bilibili under YoungYYK.**

Now I stand here, still not knowing what the road ahead holds — but at least I've recognized myself. With this as a base, I will walk step by step toward the self whose soul and body are one.

I don't know where the future leads. But I know this much: this is true.

Thank you for reading this far. If you too are someone searching for yourself in a long silence, I hope you can recognize yourself sooner — rather than taking this long to reach a starting point like I did.
