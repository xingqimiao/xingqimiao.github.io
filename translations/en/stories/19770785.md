---
title: The History of Fighting to Be Seen
---

Let me share some personal reflections. The 21st century really began with 9/11 on 11 September 2001, sharply twisting the dynamics of geopolitics and civilizational conflict. Two main forces — security fragmentation and economic integration — give this century a peculiar character distinct from the 20th. The 20th century was a century of wars and revolutions, where countless oppressed groups — the peasant and worker proletariat, ethnic minorities, the peoples of Asia, Africa and Latin America, women and sexual minorities (of course overlapping) — stepped onto the historical stage to fight for their basic "legitimacy of being seen", roaring out the bitterness of millennia of oppression through militant struggle, achieving the breakthrough from zero to one, and breaking the cages and shackles of the past. The 21st century will be a process of structural reconstruction. Those same oppressed groups still face oppression in its early phase, but their demands and methods differ from the previous century's: what they seek is not the charity of redistribution but universal recognition and acceptance; not the old one or two-element "universal standard", but de-centering and pluralism; and the ways of pursuing these demands are shifting from the political stage and the streets into the subtle details of everyday life. What will happen in this century, as always in history, is a change in the answers to questions like "Who are we?" and "How should we trust each other?".

The millennia of Chinese civilization left each of us and our elders a deep trust in legal authority, elite rule, the spirit of contract, and ideology. But that trust has proven to have big limits in actual life for us and people like us — transgender people and other minorities. The law doesn't necessarily protect the good; the Peng Yu case led to a moral crisis. The self-media era also punctured mainstream media's monopoly over the "interpretive power" of information, while trust in contract and ideology is increasingly challenged by protectionism and tightening geopolitics. Those walking in front see these challenges first, and also touch bewilderment and pain first: the old order is beginning to crumble — still faintly, but perceptibly — while the new order is nowhere in sight. Looking at Chinese history, each of the three previous great trusts took three to five centuries to form; even counting from 1840, it's less than 200 years. This is one of the sources of our pain.

Back to the present. As I said, this is a transitional period. The difficulty of transitions is that the future may be indefinitely far, and even the dawn may not come. In a transition there are those at the front and those at the back. Those in front hold newer, more pluralist ideas, but they are still in their infancy, lacking strength and voice. Those at the back are closer to the past mainstream, so they are backed by everyone who lacks the courage to move forward or doesn't accept more reasonable ideas — giving them great power and discursive authority. The world today resembles a spring in the shape of a pagoda: the back end is massive, and those in front, wanting to move forward, will inevitably be pulled backward. This abstract tug-of-war, in daily life, becomes the fierce arguments between us and our families or other people who don't understand us — or even more intense forms. But the actual disputed question is merely where this spring should sit right now. And this should be common sense: the common sense of mutual inclusion. Violating that common sense leads to a binary opposition between the transgender group and the cisgender group that should never have existed.

Our education and most of our society measure ourselves and others with an external yardstick handed down from our ancestors. They apply it everywhere, using it to attack others — that is their hatefulness. They internalize it in their minds, forgetting what they were supposed to be — that is their tragedy. What we want to do is throw away this harmful yardstick, so everyone accepts the fact that a person need not be measured by any yardstick: that the basic common sense is that a person is an independent human being before being any gender.

But for now, the overall trend is good, moving in a good direction, because the new people are walking toward transcending the labels of the past, toward independence. I didn't notice writing this much. I wonder if I went off-topic. Hope I didn't (o‿∩) — K
