---
title: Escaping Shang Jingwei
---

I am writing this to condemn the inhumane treatment I received at the Shanghai Mental Health Center, and to warn those who come after. The shadow of Shang Jingwei has never faded from me — not one bit.

This article is written entirely from my real experiences and carries the nature of a memoir. No matter how absurd a detail may seem, it truly happened. To protect the privacy of those involved, some information has been blurred; details involving self-harm, suicide, and dangerous objects are likewise rendered non-operational.

## I. Police at the Bedside

When I woke up, there were two police officers standing by my bed.

My consciousness was still floating in a thin fog. The dizziness from the previous night's medication hadn't fully cleared; the ceiling, the wardrobe, and the two unfamiliar faces all seemed to sit behind frosted glass. I stared at them for a few seconds before I understood what those uniforms meant.

Police officers appearing in a bedroom usually don't bring good news.

"Awake?" One of them called my name. "Do you know why we're here?"

"No."

"Were you posting extreme statements online again yesterday?"

I didn't answer.

In that instant, I already guessed what had happened. The suicide letter I'd sent the day before had likely been seen by someone, who passed it on to my parents or the police. During that period I had been constantly searching for highly toxic chemicals. I had severe depression, anxiety, and suicidal tendencies. Now, those thoughts hidden in search histories, chat windows, and delivery pages had finally crossed the screen and become the two people standing beside my bed.

"No," I said.

My parents stood in the doorway with the look I knew well but never wanted to face: worry, exhaustion, and a fear of not knowing what to do with me.

"Get dressed. Come with us for a ride."

"Where to?"

"The Shanghai Mental Health Center."

"Can I not go?"

"No."

There was no room to argue. I dressed and went downstairs. Two officers flanked me left and right, another walking ahead. It looked less like an escort than an escort under arrest. In the underground garage, I sat in the back seat of the police car while my parents followed in their own.

This was probably my third or fourth ride in a police car. The earlier ones also had to do with suicide. My memory of the times has blurred, leaving only similar car doors, similar silence, and the streets receding outside the window.

There were three officers in the car. The body-worn camera pointed at me. I held my phone but dared not light the screen. I had taken the road to "Jingwei" more than once, but in the past it had been the family car, a taxi, or an ambulance. Only this time, through the police car's window, I watched the familiar elevated highway, intersections, and plane trees slide past one by one.

Shanghai ran on as usual. Only my life was being carried toward a door that would lock shut behind me.

## II. Signing

The police car stopped in front of the outpatient building. The officers brought me into the emergency department, cut through the crowd, pushed to the very front of the line, and quickly conferred with the staff. I stood aside while the other two officers watched me, like guarding an object that might go out of control at any moment.

Jingwei's emergency room wasn't unfamiliar to me. Before, I had seen emotionally agitated people being brought in, shouting and screaming. I would instinctively avert my gaze back then, thinking they belonged to a world different from mine. Now I stood in the same place and suddenly realized that, in other people's eyes, I had become one of those people who need to be avoided.

The lead officer went to the "serious illness registration" window first, then after a moment walked toward "admission registration." At the sight of those words, my heart sank.

"I'm not staying in the hospital." I raised my voice. "Being admitted will only make me worse."

Nobody stopped what they were doing.

The papers were handed over one by one, the forms filled in one by one, until only the guardian's signature remained. My mother had cried herself unable to speak; her hand shook too much to hold the pen.

"Don't listen to him. Sign it," the officer said.

"No!"

The officer looked at me. "This is for your own good. We believe in science; the hospital will cure you."

"How can you be so sure?"

"Because we believe in science."

"You'll thank us when you're discharged," said one of the police.

Those words were like a needle, piercing precisely the last shred of my sanity. I glared at my parents and spoke the words most able to hurt them and most clearly my defiance, making my final struggle: "If you commit me, I will find every way to die. As soon as I'm discharged, I'll kill myself."

Both parents were crying.

The officers urged my mother to sign. Her hand trembled the whole time; my father took the pen, then, at a loss, handed it back to her. At last, my mother, as if suddenly draining her last strength, wiped away her tears.

"I'll sign. I'll sign!"

The instant the pen tip touched the paper, I knew there was no turning back.

Begging was useless, and escape was impossible. Three officers guarded the sides; I couldn't even find a single direction to break through. Desperation had nowhere to go and finally turned back onto my own body. I clawed at my wrist with my fingernails over and over until the skin broke.

"What are you doing!"

An officer noticed and grabbed my hand immediately; the others closed in. They forced my arms apart, controlled me left and right, and took me toward the inpatient building.

Along the way, people turned to look. Some gazes held curiosity, some alarm. For a moment I felt a strange thrill, as if as long as others feared me, I still possessed some power. But the feeling vanished quickly. This admission pressed down on me like a boulder, leaving no room to think of anything else.

At the inpatient building's entrance there was a checkpoint, with "No Forcing Through" painted on the wall and several security guards nearby. The elevator took us up to the twelfth floor. The elevator hall held only a steel door leading to the ward, locked; an officer rang the bell. After a while, a nurse came out and took the thick stack of papers.

"New patient?"

"Yes, all the procedures are here."

The steel door opened. I was brought inside, and the door closed behind me.

The sound wasn't loud, but it was like a stamp falling onto a long stretch of my life to come.

## III. Ward Twelve

Between the elevator hall and the ward there was still a buffer zone — glass partitions, security doors, access controls, one layer wrapped inside the next. It reminded me of a laboratory observation area: the people outside can see in, but those inside can never touch the outside world.

The admission check began with a body search. I was ordered to remove all my clothes; medical staff examined my body and took photographs for the records. My own clothes and shoes were taken away and replaced with an oversized hospital gown and plastic slippers. Nothing could be kept; nothing truly belonged to me; only this body of mine was left.

The ward was more dilapidated than I had imagined. The wall plaster was peeling off in large chunks, exposing cold grey concrete; the walls bore dark stains from dampness, muddy in colour, like some stain that could never be washed clean. Another wall was covered with scrawled writing — some still barely readable, some with no connection to one another, as if many people had left their broken thoughts there.

My bed was near the door, far from the window. The little sunlight and cityscape outside couldn't reach me. Iron bars were on the window, and no extra objects were to be found in the room. The air held a stale, damp smell, mixed with disinfectant and the breath of a place long unventilated.

I only learned later that I had been assigned Level 1 nursing care — the highest level here.

After changing clothes, I was led into the corridor. On both sides stood rows of restraint chairs: wooden, heavy, with armrests, and a chest board that could fold down and lock. Once you sat in one and the board came down, it was very hard to stand again. Fellow patients sat quietly in their own places — mostly middle-aged and older; the young were few, and minors like me even rarer. Their gaze was uniformly sluggish; they only lifted their heads to glance at me as I passed, then looked away.

I too was locked into one of those chairs. The armrest behind me was broken, hollow inside, filled with dust, and there were cockroaches crawling about. One of them crawled onto my chair; I could only lean as far forward as I could and pray it wouldn't climb onto me.

"Restrain him."

Two cloth straps bound my wrists, fixing my hands to the armrests.

That was around three in the afternoon. Until six in the evening, I sat there. No phone, no book, no conversation, nothing to occupy my attention. I could only look at the wall, at the lamplight falling on the floor tiles, at nurses and patients in yellow vests passing back and forth.

Time for the first time showed its true, terrifying shape. It wasn't the numbers on a clock, but a blankness without boundaries. Inside it, a person can neither advance nor retreat; they can only stay awake, waiting for the next minute to repeat the last.

When dinner came, I didn't eat a bite, and I refused the water too. The food looked even worse than school meals. Refusing food was one of the very few ways to control that I could still find.

The nurse warned me: "If you don't eat, we'll have to take medical measures."

"I don't care."

She asked me at least to drink water. I kept refusing, and in the end water was forced into my mouth. I coughed a few times; swallowing was like a failed resistance.

That evening came the blood draws. Because I was newly admitted, there were many tests — one tube after another. The needle hurt, but I was too weary to react. I noticed that some of the people pushing the meal carts, moving supplies, and tidying things were not medical staff at all, but fellow patients in yellow vests. They seemed to know this order better than I did, and understood when to lower their heads and when to stay silent.

At nine the lights went out. The restraints on my wrists had just been removed when I was taken back to the bed, my four limbs strapped separately to the bed frame. My body lay forced open, unable to turn over. The discomfort of medication withdrawal and the stress of a new admission piled on top of each other — and above my head hung a fluorescent light that never went out through the night.

I stared at the wall with open eyes, unable to fall asleep.

I thought of my life before, of what crime I had committed, why I had ended up here. Grievance, rage, and fear churned through my body over and over, until all that was left was a very faint crying. I didn't dare cry too loud; it felt as though even sorrow would be registered and evaluated, then answered with harsher handling.

Around three in the morning I finally lost consciousness. At five, the nursing assistant woke me.

## IV. Fixed Time

In the early morning, still not awake, I was roused loudly by the nurse, my mind blank. The assistant undid the bed restraints, but the straps stayed on my wrists. I dragged them along to brush my teeth and wash my face; every motion became clumsy. When that was done, I was taken back to the corridor, into that chair, and strapped in again. I propped my heavy, sleepy head up with one hand and leaned forward as far as I could to keep from touching the cockroaches on the back of the chair.

Breakfast came at six. I still refused to eat. When the assistant took away the untouched bowl of porridge, he glanced at me. I couldn't tell whether it was anger or disgust.

That morning I was taken for a chest X-ray. When we left the ward, my hands were bound together and led by a strap. The special elevator descended; the doors opened and different light and voices appeared at the end of the corridor. Looking back later, that short trip to radiology counted as the most "interesting" experience I had in Ward Twelve. How bored must a person be to treat a chest X-ray as a break?

Back to the ward, and sitting again. No lunch, no dinner. The medical staff began giving me fluids. The clear liquid dripped down slowly along the line; I watched it enter my veins, without resistance and without gratitude. The second night, I stared at the wall again. On the third day, because I had kept not eating, I received more examinations and fluids. The overnight infusion somehow caused my blood sugar to spike abnormally the next day.

I no longer knew whom I was punishing. At first I thought the hunger strike was a fight against the hospital, my parents, and the form I had signed; only later did I understand that the first to be drained was always me. Yet I also felt I deserved it — so stupid, to have let a mental illness and suicidal tendencies be seen by others. I, of all people, was best at hiding emotions.

It was garbage-collection time. The assistant piled all kinds of waste in the corridor and dragged out a stinking bedsheet — the work of the seventy- or eighty-year-old patient next door, who had lost all ability to care for himself and had defecated on the sheet. For half an hour of cleaning, even for the hour after, the whole corridor reeked of faeces and decay.

That afternoon, a new patient came to Ward Ten. He was angrier than anyone here, insisting he was perfectly fine — that he'd merely lost his temper with his family once and was sent here. Actually, based on how he described what happened, I even felt his anger was entirely reasonable. He kept trying to break free, shouting and making a scene. Nobody paid him any attention.

That night, the new patient roared all night, shaking his bed, demanding to call the police. I couldn't fall asleep at all.

The third afternoon, my parents came to visit. The moment they walked into the ward, my hands still bound, sitting on the bed, I burst into tears.

That was probably the most disheveled I had cried in my sixteen years. I couldn't explain why I was crying. I wept until I couldn't breathe, saying over and over that I couldn't take it; tears and snot soaked the bedsheet. My parents, seeing me like that, promised to try to move me to a better ward.

I got my phone back briefly and immediately messaged a few people I was close to: "I've been committed against my will. I'm going crazy." There followed many more words about death. I wasn't asking for help then — at least not subjectively; I more wanted to end myself to escape the torment. But looking back now, every message I sent out was, in some sense, waiting for someone to catch me.

After the visit ended, I was taken to a meeting room about twenty medical students sat inside, each with pen and paper. At the head of the long table sat a chief physician and an assisting physician. I was placed beside them, restraint straps still on my wrists.

The assisting physician read out my medical history aloud: depression, gender-identity-related distress, drug abuse, and several suicide risk events. The things that had only been said in whispers in a consulting room were read out item by item, turned into material for everyone to record.

The chief physician began to question me. From my upbringing to what had happened recently; from why I depended on medication to why I wanted to become a girl; from when that wish began to why I wanted to die. One question after another, taking me apart piece by piece into symptoms, triggers, a timeline, and diagnostic grounds.

To some questions I told the truth; to others I lied. I kept my expression as calm as possible. I had once been good at debating and knew how to organize language under pressure, and how to give answers that sounded reasonable. That little ability became my last armor in that moment.

The chief physician delivered the analysis; the students bent their heads to write. I, however, understood clearly: in this moment I was not only a patient but a case — a lesson plan unfolding live. Truly worthy of the country's best psychiatric hospital.

That night, I had the first glimmer of hope. My parents said I might be moved to Ward Fourteen the next day.

Around ten at night, I still couldn't sleep, sitting on the bed looking at the mottled wall. My entire time in Ward Twelve, no doctor had come to understand my condition, no one had prescribed antidepressants or sleeping pills; they'd simply locked me in here. There was no treatment effect at all — instead it left me with deep psychological trauma. Is this what the so-called "most advanced" psychiatric care looks like?

## V. Ward Fourteen

The next day I woke at five as usual and forced down a few spoonfuls of porridge. At noon, finding my mood somewhat stabilized, the doctors completed the handover between Ward Twelve and Ward Fourteen, and I was taken upstairs.

Ward Fourteen was called the "VIP ward." The moment I walked in, I felt an almost absurd contrast: intact walls, soft lighting, less murky air — it finally felt like an ordinary hospital. The restraints were removed, I changed back into my own clothes, I could shower every day, use my phone, and even have family order meals for me.

If I'd never been to Ward Twelve, these might have been the most ordinary details of life; coming out of there, they felt like luxuries.

I used my phone vindictively, like taking back every minute I'd lost. My parents ordered me a wagyu rice bowl. The first mouthful went down, and I realized I hadn't truly eaten in a long time. My stomach protested, but my sense of taste felt reborn. That meal ended my near-week-long hunger strike.

My new attending doctor was surnamed Chen. She would remain my doctor afterwards. She asked many questions, yet her tone stayed gentle throughout, never treating each statement as a danger signal needing immediate subduing. She reworked the treatment plan, prescribing sedation, anti-anxiety, and sleep-improving medication.

At that moment I thought: this is what I imagined "Jingwei" should have been.

The next day, a tablet was brought in. I could listen to music, scroll, and watch anime — superficially reconnected to my old life. But what Ward Twelve left in me didn't vanish because the environment got better. When a lock clicked, when a nurse's footsteps quickened, when someone came close to the bed, I tensed up instantly. My body remembered, ahead of my mind, the feel of being pinned down, bound, and of staring at a wall all night.

I began chatting frequently with someone online. She was about my age, also struggling with gender identity and depression, and a champion in chemistry competitions. She was out of town then, and I kept asking her for sodium azide. I described death like a concrete plan, as if a sufficiently rigorous plan would let me reclaim control of my fate.

A few days later she returned to Shanghai and came to see me in the hospital at once. That was our first offline meeting. She gave me much comfort, and also brought the thing I had asked her for. To this day I am still deeply grateful to her.

Before leaving the ward, she secretly told the nurse about my suicidal intent.

The staff acted immediately. I was strapped to the bed again, and my phone was taken. In that instant, the memories of Ward Twelve flooded back. I was terrified, furious, and felt betrayed. The doctors searched the room thoroughly, but never found the small paper-wrapped pouch of sodium nitrite hidden in my trouser pocket.

That night, during a gap between rounds, I swallowed it.

Sodium nitrite goes down cold and smooth, slightly salty, with a hint of sweetness. My heart rate climbed, my head felt light, then turned to a headache. My heart pounded harder; I felt palpitations and anxiety, my breathing quickened, my consciousness blurred, my body lost its strength, and slight convulsions began. The nurses noticed something was wrong, and the ward instantly became noisy. Someone called a doctor, someone readied oxygen, someone phoned for an ambulance. I could hear the sounds, but my brain had lost the capacity to understand them.

And then the lights, the corridor, and the faces all broke off.

## VI. After Midnight

Around midnight on May 11, I was admitted to the intensive care unit at Zhongshan Hospital.

Consciousness briefly floated up, as if a last flash before the end. I felt many instruments and tubes being quickly attached to my body. I heard doctors and nurses talking at the rescue bed: my skin was turning purple, my limbs convulsing, my vital signs very poor, methemoglobin exceeding the instrument's upper limit — 70 percent.

I couldn't respond. Couldn't speak, couldn't move my fingers, couldn't even blink. Breathing no longer fully belonged to me.

Someone said my convulsions needed sedatives. Then the voices drifted away again.

A coma is not sleep. Sleep may still have dreams, a murky sense of time; a coma has nothing. Not even darkness, because darkness is still a sensation. There was only a void impossible to describe. By the time I had consciousness again, two days had passed.

Around three in the morning on May 13, I woke up.

The moment I woke, I couldn't control my own body. A tube was in my windpipe; catheters ran through my nose and body. Every breath brought a foreign sensation; the vapor of my breath condensed and flowed back along the tube wall, like drowning and choking. When a nurse cleared my secretions, a thin tube went deep into my throat; the discomfort nearly broke me again.

After seven in the morning, a doctor judged my vital signs essentially stable and gradually removed the tracheal tube and other catheters. My hands were still strapped to the bed, mainly to prevent accidents before my consciousness fully returned.

After surviving, what came first was not epiphany but boredom.

My parents and teachers came to visit; seeing me awake, they all visibly relaxed. When the visit ended, I could only lie in bed. With nothing to do, I borrowed a book from a nurse — "ICU Ultrasound Manual," the only book here — and read it. A doctor asked which middle school I attended and how my grades were. I mentioned my middle-school exam scores and my experience in physics and informatics competitions. They praised me as clever, saying I looked like a genius.

That was the first time since entering high school that I had heard such words again. Lying on the ICU bed, just back from death's edge, I felt a faint excitement at a long-missed affirmation. Now that I think of it, the excitement was both absurd and real. A sixteen-year-old's self-esteem can always revive in the most inappropriate places.

On the 14th, my parents sent me an e-reader and a paperback novel, "Super Kaguya-hime." I killed time reading, often until late at night. The characters in the story crossed worlds and were reborn, while I lay in the sound of the monitors, turning pages one by one, temporarily not thinking about why I was still alive.

On the morning of the 15th, the doctor announced I was out of danger and could leave the ICU.

The ambulance took me back to the Shanghai Mental Health Center.

## VII. Leaving

Because I had just attempted suicide, after returning to Ward Fourteen I was again under strict restraint. My hands and feet were strapped to the bed; my range of movement was only a little space around the mattress.

I can no longer recall precisely how long that period lasted. My memory was sliced into similar fragments by medication, sleep, and a repeat routine. Every day: reading, scrolling videos, receiving ward rounds, occasionally talking to my parents. Compared to Ward Twelve, conditions were far better — but "far better" does not equal bearable.

My mood deteriorated bit by bit. Losing control of my own body for so long made me more and more agitated. The more the staff feared I would hurt myself, the more they needed to restrain me; the stricter the restraint, the more I felt I was no longer a person but a risk; the angrier and more defiant I became, the more convinced they were that the restraint was necessary. We were trapped in a loop that kept proving itself to each other.

Finally, one day I woke up and could bear it no longer. I fought loudly with my parents, insisting on leaving. I began undoing the straps myself. A nurse noticed, called the assistants, tied me up again, pushed my bed out to the nurse's station in the corridor, and put protective gloves on me.

I kept struggling, even managing to pull the gloves off. Assistants came to control me; I fought desperately. Someone warned that if I continued, they'd use sedatives. The argument grew fiercer, and finally the head nurse stepped in and called my parents aside.

I saw my parents crying again.

The first time, at the admission signing, they wept as they sent me in; this time, they wept as they agreed to let me out. Between those two sets of tears lay the steel door of Ward Twelve, the window of Ward Fourteen, the ICU, and one death almost fully carried out.

The next day, the procedures were done. I changed into my own clothes, got my belongings back, and followed my parents through the ward's access gates into the elevator. The numbers fell floor by floor. Walking out of the inpatient building, I learned what it felt like to be released from prison.

At last I had left Jingwei.

But leaving was not like an escape in the movies. No running, no pursuit, no victory music. I just walked beside my parents, slowly, toward the parking spot. The city was as noisy as ever; vehicles still passed through the intersection; everything was the same as on the day I was brought here.

For a long time, I understood the whole experience as pure imprisonment. I remember the name I was forced to sign, the iron doors and restraint straps, the light burning all night, and how my privacy became a case file in front of a room full of strangers. Nor can I deny that someone later noticed my abnormality in the ward, someone gave me oxygen in the ambulance, someone pulled me back from the brink in the ICU.

My parents said I'd thank them after discharge. I didn't suddenly thank everything as they'd hoped, nor did surviving automatically make me love life. The thought of death didn't vanish on discharge day, and the fear the wards left behind didn't end with the closing of the steel door. Real recovery takes much longer than one rescue; it is far more complex than the four characters "believe in science."

What "escaping" came to mean for me later has another layer. It wasn't merely leaving a building, nor beating the doctors, nurses, police, or parents. It was slowly stepping back from that dead end where the only way to prove you still had choices was to hurt yourself; it was admitting that the sixteen-year-old me was both furious and afraid, both wanting to die and, time after time, still sending messages to others; it was allowing the person who had been strapped to a bed not to remain forever on that bed.

To this day, when falling asleep, I still feel the near-death sensation of swallowing salt. I still dream of Ward Twelve's corridor. In the dream, the steel door locks behind me, the light is pale white, and time stands still. I look for an exit again and again, and each time I wake to find that outside the window, it is already dawn.

I write this not to prove how close I came to death, nor to dress my suffering in a medal worth showing off. I only want to give that chaotic memory a shape, and to give myself — the one who was never genuinely heard back then — one full chance to speak.

I did leave Jingwei in the end.

But my soul died inside it.
