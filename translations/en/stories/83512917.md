## The "Main Text"?

### Early Childhood

#### Spawn

According to legend, I came into this world in a room inside my second uncle's company. I don't know if he has moved away by now; in any case, I've roughly completely forgotten where the room was.

I was born in a small county town in Shandong. The pace of life was neither fast nor slow, just right: no small-village acquaintance society, no big-city life pressure. Everything seemed just right.

My family first lived in the innermost building of our own company. Every day I played in the little garden of my father's company. The employees loved playing with me.

Sadly, a new office building was later built over that little garden (it's actually had quite a few years on it now), and the house we first lived in has likely been converted into a warehouse or something. Many of the people I once knew have left or retired. Some old employees are still there — but many of them I've forgotten who's who. Yet they remember me.

#### Third-Aunt Grandma

Everyone has their own "morning flowers, evening harvest", right?

After that, for a period I was sent to live with Third-Aunt Grandma in the countryside.

That was probably the happiest time of my life.

Although I could already remember things then, now it's all very vague. I've been back a few times in recent years, and Third-Aunt Grandma told me bits and pieces of that time. One thing she told me that stuck deeply: one night I was crying and making noise; the adults sneaked a corn from someone else's field, and when I got home I pestered to eat it, so they steamed it for me to gnaw on. At that time I loved playing with a certain older girl; once she had me try roller-skating, and even then I could only glide a little while holding her hand.

Funny thing: I naively thought I had only one sister, and after leaving, I envied everyone else for having a real older sister to play with every day. My understanding then: having a sister was the happiest thing in the world. ~~Until I later learned that my first-uncle's house had a female cousin.~~

Back then I also learned to drive an electric tricycle (yes — the kind you see all over third-tier cities and villages); it was one of my proudest achievements. Once we went to the fields to irrigate, and on the way back a few farmers passed on their tricycle and greeted us with:

> "Hey! Is a little kid allowed to drive the car?" (Shandong accent)
> Third-Aunt Grandma: "Sure, sure, he's real good at it" (Shandong accent)

When my mother came to take me back to the city, I even drove her to the market.

Who is Third-Aunt Grandma? Even now I've never sorted out what relation she is to me, and I've even forgotten her name. Growing up, I've never had a right opportunity — or rather, never knew how to bring it up.

### Primary School

My family should count as petty bourgeois: my father is a merchant, and my mother is quite a virtuous wife. Income was considerable. For peers of the same class, schooling could be handled casually, muddle through for the degree, take over dad's business, buy an apartment, marry someone — and their whole life would be stable.

But it happened that **my father and I** both didn't want me to live this life out so blandly.

> If you don't take a bite, you'll never know if it tastes like a piece of shit.

Remember these words, because they drove most of my decisions later.

#### Normal University Affiliated Primary School

I clearly remember that morning: my father woke me early; after breakfast, I thought we'd go to kindergarten as usual. But the car didn't turn left like usual as we went out the gate — it turned right, against the norm. Were we going to see Grandpa? Soon the car stopped, not having gone far.

I was taken to a strange place: primary school. Walking through the gate, my heart pounded — pure fear of the new environment. I was taken to a room (classroom), surrounded entirely by kids about my size, and then Dad left.

I don't know how long I was afraid, or what I was like when I got home; in any case, I began a childhood which, at the time, I thought was not very good.

I first attended the preschool class, and I was even the class monitor. Nothing much to brag about.

One impression stayed very strong: I secretly brought a little toy to school (I've forgotten what it was — probably one of those toys from a Kinder Surprise egg). The teacher found it, threw it out the window. There were iron bars on our classroom window, so even though we were on the first floor, I could only watch my toy lying out there with other scattered toys. I kept it on my mind for half a semester.

Another time, I sharpened a very sharp pencil and waved it around the class like a sword. Carelessly, the lead jabbed right into my arm. To this day you can still faintly see that scar.

Another time, the whole family went out for teppanyaki. I picked up my cup — unaware it was my cousin's beer glass. I took a big gulp and nearly threw up; I never imagined beer tasted like this.

That day had more beyond that: after dinner, the cousins were play-fighting outside the door. I poked my head out a little and was knocked flat by a cousin dashing toward me, my head banged against the door lock, leaving a scar.

Actually, I don't dislike this scar. In a certain sense, maybe it's a unique marker of mine (？)

This was the most beautiful time of my entire primary school. Because, in the following six years, I held the position of last in class.

My parents later said that **because I hadn't attended a bridging class**, I was sent to school a year early, so I didn't have advantages in various aspects. Of course this conflicts with what I said just now. I don't know which is right.

At that time this school had an extremely high reputation among locals. The school had two principals; one was my father's classmate (though I never mentioned it, I think I got in through his connection to my father). That vice-principal also taught us politics (called "Morality and Life" back then).

The other principal looked past fifty — a classic Mediterranean hairline, clearly a man not to be messed with.

Around fourth grade, once I was play-fighting with someone. The corridor was full of kids, noisy — but he spotted us at a glance. I noticed the principal first and frantically alerted the other guy; he didn't see, and the principal was already walking toward us. At the last moment he saw the principal and dashed back into the classroom with a "whoosh".

The principal surely saw, so he called the kid out at the classroom door. I tried poking my head out to a peek — **and got a hard kick from that person.** I completely couldn't understand what that was for. Then, as usual: lecture us first, then have the class monitor take us to the homeroom teacher, who lectured us again. What the monitor told the teacher — I remember it exactly:

> "The two of them were running around in the corridor and got caught by the principal."
> ~~"What the hell, we were play-wrestling, not running."~~
> In the end the punishment was copying classical poems.

I will never forget that kick and that line: *"The two of them were running around in the corridor and got caught by the principal."*

In short, because of my grades and my ~~so-called~~ naughtiness, I became a "bad kid" in the teachers' minds.

Homework lasted past midnight every day; too many errors in dictation meant a butt-spanking; during the day I was a thorn in the teachers' eyes. During those Normal University-Affiliate years, even in a third-grade class of over 40 kids, I made only two friends — and I hadn't even learned everyone's names by the end of the semester. One I met in grade 1; his father worked at my father's company, so we're still in touch — my childhood buddy. The other, met in grade 3; no contact since. Though I wasn't quite a lone wolf, the group games children play — like house — I basically never joined.

#### Moving House

We moved twice. The first time, from the company to a housing estate: a three-bedroom, one-living-room flat (3LDK in Japanese terms). That was probably before kindergarten.

Likely because of my younger brother's and sister's births, the three-bedroom got crowded. Around fourth grade, my father bought a personal iron bed and had me sleep in the living room. Since that wasn't a long-term plan, my father also bought a detached four-story villa on the east side of the city, and the whole family except me moved there. I didn't move immediately because of school; instead, they brought my grandfather over to keep me company. Later my grandfather returned to his old place, and I moved into the new house — for the last stretch of fourth grade, I took a taxi to school every day.

#### Transferring Schools

That summer of fourth grade, my father sat with me in the yard discussing transferring. He said he'd found three more schools, all outside the city, and asked if I'd go. I hesitated for a while, then agreed.

All three schools had entrance exams; the second one also had an interview.

On the first sitting, I failed all three.

Afterwards, I tried again for the second school (HS).

In my second sitting, a teacher walked over to interview me. He sat across from me, picked up the ruler set I'd brought, took out the straight ruler and the 60° set-square, stood the ruler upright, placed the triangle atop it — forming an arrow shape pointing north — and asked me: "What does this look like?" I looked, and instinctively said: "Like a tree." He smiled, then turned the whole shape sideways into an arrow pointing right, and asked again what it looked like. He might have been nudging me toward saying "an arrow", but I didn't catch on, thought for a moment and said: "Like a fallen tree." Only after he repeated the question did I realize it was an arrow.

This teacher was very special; a large part of his influence still shapes who I am now.

But anyway, I passed, and from September started fifth grade. Later I heard it was my father's banquet table and money-power doing the work.

#### HS

#### The Remaining Two Years of Childhood

The new school was in Weifang's high-tech zone — a true arrival "in the city", for me. The school was boarding. Yes: I started boarding in fifth grade.

What made me happy: I was finally no longer last in class. But not much higher — I was second from last. And in group assignments, I often got paired with the last-place kid.

I still hadn't changed: if someone noticed me, I'd play with them; if not, I stayed alone...

#### Brother Quan

Why call him "Brother Quan"? It was the school's habit, or tradition: calling teachers "Brother Quan" or "Sister Ying" and the like — quite a gangster feel. Nobody calls them that anymore now.

Brother Quan was our grade director — also the math teacher of the next class plus its "chief mentor". (In this school, homeroom teachers aren't called homeroom teachers; they're called "chief mentors".) Age unknown; he was a middle-aged man with black-rimmed glasses. In my impression, he was a stern teacher, and I was very afraid of him. Fortunately he didn't teach us in fifth grade — but when sixth grade came, because grades 3 and 4 were a paired class group, he was assigned as our math teacher, too.

I remember once in sixth grade I had an unexpected stellar showing and did better than usual. He called me out, grabbed me by the collar of my jacket, dragged me to the boys' toilet's washstand, and demanded:

> "So you CAN score well, huh?! Huh?! And what did that score you gave me before mean?! Talk!"

At the Normal University-Affiliate school, when a teacher interrogated me, I couldn't and didn't dare say a word. But here, I was required to answer an interrogation; otherwise it was disrespecting the teacher. Later I found that in China, any superior demands this of subordinates — which left me especially unskilled at it. **Because when I heard that tone of voice, I was already scared stiff, to the point of nearly crying. Sometimes the teacher would deliberately keep me there because I said nothing — which made me desperately afraid.**

This man was no simple case. Rumor had it he was the then-principal's child, getting his positions as math teacher + grade director through connections. Rumors said the Class 5 math teacher even had to make his slides and script for his classes. Later in middle school the principal changed, and he left with the job — opening a tutoring class outside. During our high-school entrance exam, some classmates went to his class for final sprinting. The last time I saw him was on exam day, at the exam-site entrance, standing outside.

#### Sister Ying

She was my math teacher and our class's chief mentor, teaching me all of fifth grade's math. She came from the same county town as me.

She wasn't chief mentor from day one: first a middle-school teacher came to teach us math, transferred away within days; then the Class 1 teacher taught us a lesson or two; finally Sister Ying joined and taught us math.

That was probably the happiest stretch of my elementary years. Sister Ying was gentle, taught carefully — the kind of teacher who truly understands children. Most importantly, she never interrogated me in a towering rage. Even when I made mistakes, she used a soft tone.

One day I came to class late. When she asked why, I suddenly felt dizzy — nothing serious, honestly — but she still sent me to the infirmary, then took me to the office to rest. Brother Quan was in the office; fearing I'd be scared, she settled me outside and told me not to look at Brother Quan. Then she went back to her work. Once I'd recovered a bit, I went back to class.

Another day I forgot my schoolbag on the way to school — to me, a capital offense. One day in the Normal University-Affiliate school, without a bag, I'd been stopped by security. But she didn't lose her temper as I imagined. Although she was on the whole a strict teacher, in my memory there's no such scene of her at all.

#### Dare to Duck Round with Me?

I forget when this was — maybe sixth grade or seventh grade. One night at home, unable to sleep on my side, I suddenly had the thought of wearing a skirt. But as the eldest son, if there was a skirt in the house it would either be my mother's or my little sister's. Mom's was too big; my sister's, too small.

Still, I got up, off the bed, went into the next room's dressing area, and finally found the skirt my sister had worn during some Chinese New Year of a year unknown.

I stood before the mirror, held the skirt up in front of me for a try... then immediately put it back and returned to my room.

Not because I feared being discovered — but because I disgusted myself.

### Middle School, and That Teacher

One day in the second semester of sixth grade, going upstairs back to the dormitory, a teacher suddenly called my name from behind. I thought: who is this, how do they know my name? Turning around, it took me three or four seconds to recognize the teacher who had interviewed me. If you've forgotten him, I mentioned him in the [#Transfers](#transferring-schools) section. After a greeting, I didn't think much more of it.

Entering middle school, I walked into the classroom with trepidation — and there he was, talking with some parents. Yes: he was my middle school's chief mentor. And he taught me for all three years.

We called him Brother Xian.

#### Brother Xian

Brother Xian was much more intense than Brother Quan. Brother Quan at worst pinched your skin — mostly scaring you. But Brother Xian would really put his hands on you...

He also taught politics to the whole grade. He was a Party member. Though strict and frightening, he was also a reasonable man, with his own moral code.

In seventh grade, a classmate was merely playing with his phone in the dorm. The dorm manager found it and turned him in. Brother Xian beat him up. The beating happened outside; we were in the classroom, and every hit made everyone's heart leap. He smashed the phone with no mercy at all. Whether that classmate later paid compensation, I never learned.

#### "Collective"?

The collectivism so many people hate today ran through my middle school years. For a short period, after our afternoon nap, we had to stand and sing the class song. What the "class song" was I've forgotten; I only remember it was quite an old song, with lyrics about collectivism or friendship.

One day we sang poorly — not loud enough, I think — so we were ordered to stand as punishment the whole afternoon.

That day the first class was physics, and we went to the lab. The teacher told us to sit. The others didn't sit. Only I sat.

After class I put my things away and went out to the toilet. When I came back, Brother Xian was already waiting at the door. Before I could even react, a force kicked me from behind to the ground. My mind went entirely blank.

> Brother Xian: "Why did you sit down?! No one else sat but you sat?! Why are you SO special?! Speak!"
> Me: "The physics teacher... told us to sit..."
> Brother Xian: "You listen to anyone's word, don't you?! What right do you have, huh?!
> I'm telling you: besides MY words, you don't need to listen to anyone. Even if the principal comes, he listens to me!"

I got beaten. Then he let the others sit, and made me and one other stand for the whole afternoon...

> "I'm telling you: besides MY words, you don't need to listen to anyone. Even if the principal comes, he listens to me!"

#### Growth Diary

At this school you had to write a "growth diary" — primary, middle, and high school all required it.

To be specific, the growth diary is: "a paperbound volume that the school — or the education group — forcibly requires every student to write in daily, in order to serve their so-called certain 'education models'".

Though named a "diary", the school required you to write tomorrow's plan — like a paper-based ToDo list.

Usually the diary has a fixed format, depending on different leaders' requirements and different periods. For example, the first two lines asked for the morning-reading recitation plan — precise to which hours, what to recite, which page of the textbook, to what degree of mastery (~~how "well-done"~~). If you missed a day's plan, you'd be required to make it up. Even if the morning-reading period had already passed, you still had to write the so-called "plan".

I hated writing it intensely. As long as no one checked mine, I left it blank. By the second half of the semester, I had quite a lot of blanks. That was when the teacher, to face the [supervision inspection](#supervision), basically chose to staple the unwritten parts up and had me symbolically write a few — and that was that.

#### Supervision

The school belonged to an education group; the group generally sent people to every school for a check in the first part of the semester and the last part. These people formed a department called the "Supervision Team".

From the school down to every small group, periodic or irregular checks of subordinate departments' functioning — partly to keep the school running normally, and partly to handle the two supervision inspections. Utterly absurd.

We probably met in seventh grade. In my memory, the beginning of seventh grade we got along decently; later we hardly spoke; then we got along again.

#### Little Horse and the Piano

Little Horse was a boy from another class I got on well with in seventh grade. He loved playing the piano. I knew a bit of piano back then, learned over a summer — honestly, I could only play "Castle in the Sky". I never played it again, and Little Horse transferred schools too.

#### ZTZJ

She was the first girl I ever liked. Even now about all that's left are memories I can't bear to look at.

I still remember my first love letter: I didn't dare give it myself, and had someone else give it to her. What was I thinking then?

Sometimes when my finger or something got nicked and bled a little, I'd write love words in blood. (Which she never got.)

When I first learned online shopping, I bought a few pocket watches; I gave one to her. When I had it customized, I even put her photo on the back cover.

#### The Boy Who Couldn't Sit at a Table

Around seventh grade, the only friend I had in the entire class was [Rui](#rui). Our class had three tables assigned in the cafeteria, and everyone had the habit of clustering — so I got squeezed out of two tables. The third table was where the girls clustered; going there would probably get me called a pervert ().

Fortunately, I had some classmates in other classes with whom I wasn't on bad terms, so I went to eat at their tables. But the good times were short — the other classes' chief mentors gradually forbade class-hopping for meals (seemingly... aimed at me).

Several times I had absolutely nowhere to sit. I leaned against the railing by the stairs watching the evening news; if I didn't want to look too awkward, I'd go get a second serving of food. Eventually I even found a trick: if today's food was hard to grab, I'd rush in, get a portion, and see if there were free seats; if not, I'd fake a second serving until a seat opened. If the food was mediocre, I'd wait until later to eat.

#### First Attempt at Buying Women's Clothes?

I learned online shopping in middle school and tried buying little things — an Air China badge, a few refined pocket watches, one of which I gave to [ZTZJ](#ztzj).

Later I used Pinduoduo to buy a JK uniform — pink. When it arrived, I dashed to my room and put it on.

That was my first time wearing women's clothes. I wasn't used to it; it felt drafty down there.

The car back to school was almost here, and I didn't have much time. Unwillingly, I hurriedly changed and went downstairs.

### Year One of High School

Shaken but unhurt, I entered high school — then came the most willful period of my student life.

My first-year homeroom teacher was a history teacher. Perhaps his teaching career wasn't perfect, but he truly taught me many things about being a person.

Once on my way home from school, I borrowed a phone charger from him. I finished using it, then left it on the charging cabinet. When I came back on the weekend, the charger had vanished into thin air. I checked the surveillance video for a long time and still couldn't find who stole it.

The teacher asked me to compensate. Unfortunately I didn't have much allowance then, and my allowance management was still fairly strict. In the end I chose to bear it alone. Thinking back: I lost nothing — instead it was the heaviest, most valuable lesson of my life, more useful than any other form.

### Creating NO CONVERSION THERAPY

I had this idea not long after I was taken to the conversion institution, and once I reached Shanghai I turned it into reality step by step — powered by enormous energy, mixed with hate.

I didn't pin much hope on it, but in a let-me-try mood, I published the site: https://nct.hosinoneko.me

### Homelessing

The good times didn't last. On April 15, 2026 — a period when I was showing my father around Shanghai — there was a knock at the door in the morning. Police.

That was serious. Came within a hair's breadth of sleeping on the street twice: once the very same day (someone on Telegram offered me temporary lodging), and once after they could no longer take me in for some reason (on that occasion I received aid through X again).
