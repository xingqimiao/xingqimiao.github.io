## What does it mean to be transgender?

            Every person has a deep sense of their own identity. For most people, their gender identity matches the sex recorded on their birth certificate. But some people were assigned a sex at birth that does not match who they truly are. Trans, or transgender, describes people whose gender identity does not align with the sex they were assigned at birth. This includes, for example, a person assigned male at birth who identifies as a woman — or as neither a man nor a woman.

            Like everyone else, transgender people are entitled to legal rights over their bodies, including the right to legal recognition of their self-identified gender. Many transgender people transition — socially, legally, or medically — in order to align their lives with their gender identity. For many, transition clearly improves their physical and mental health and quality of life.

## What are gender identity and gender expression?

            **Gender identity** is a person's deep inner sense of their own gender. It may or may not match the sex assigned at birth. Most people identify as women or men, but throughout history every culture has included people who identify between or beyond the binary.

            **Gender expression** is how a person shows their gender through outward cues such as clothing, hairstyle, makeup, chosen name, or pronouns. Some express their gender in the same way at all times; others change their expression over time, depending on their circumstances or how they feel.

## What is gender transition?

            Some transgender people choose to transition — a process of changing aspects of their life to align with their gender identity. Because everyone's needs differ, there is no single "right" way to transition. Transgender people are free to decide on their own what to change and when, according to what they need.

- Social: being openly transgender, using a new name and pronouns, and changing the way you express your gender (e.g. clothing and hairstyle).

- Legal: changing the name and/or gender marker on official documents.

- Medical: hormone replacement therapy and relevant surgeries.

## Is being transgender a disease?

            No — being transgender is not a disease; it is a natural part of human diversity. UN human rights experts note that treating transgender people as "sick" (i.e. pathologizing them) is one of the root causes of human rights violations against them. The World Health Organization also stresses that "transgender and gender-diverse identities are not conditions of mental illness; classifying them as such causes severe stigmatization." The WHO removed trans-related diagnoses from the chapter on mental disorders in the 11th revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11).

## What is transphobia?

            Transphobia is any form of prejudice or hostility towards transgender people because of their gender identity or gender expression. It can take the form of criminalization, violence, discrimination, pathologization, harassment, exclusion, stigmatization, or denial of who they are.

## The challenges

### 1. Criminalization

            Some countries criminalize transgender and gender-diverse people through laws about so-called "cross-dressing", targeting, harassing, arbitrarily arresting, and prosecuting them.

### 2. Lack of legal recognition

            The lack of legal recognition reinforces stigma and leaves transgender people more exposed to sexual and other forms of violence, hate crimes, harassment, humiliation, and abuse. Without accurate identity documents, everyday activities (going to school, renting a home, looking for a job, seeing a doctor) can be severely hindered, since they would have to reveal their transgender identity to strangers.

### 3. Violence and discrimination

            Around the world, transgender people face a higher risk of violence and discrimination. The Trans Murder Monitoring (TMM) project recorded more than 5,040 murders of transgender people worldwide between 2008 and 2024 — roughly one person killed every single day.

### 4. Barriers to healthcare

            After experiences of discrimination, or because of high medical costs, transgender people often delay the medical care they need. Using hormones without medical advice or supervision significantly increases the risk of poor health outcomes.

### 5. Transgender children and adolescents

            Research shows that transgender and gender-diverse children and adolescents are more likely to be bullied at school. Because of stigma, discrimination, and violence, they are also more likely to attempt suicide than their cisgender peers.

## Take action! What can we do?

- Learn about the experiences of transgender people and the problems and concerns they face. Show understanding and empathy.

- Listen to how people describe their identity, and use the terms, pronouns, gender, and names they use for themselves, out of respect for their inherent dignity.

- Firmly push back against discriminatory language, negative myths, and stereotypes about transgender people, and encourage people around you to do the same.

- Speak up when you witness stigmatization, discrimination, or violence against transgender people.

- Respect privacy: do not make assumptions about anyone's gender identity, and do not disclose it without their consent.

- Advocate, at official, media, and educational levels, for laws and policies that recognize transgender identities and fight stigma.
