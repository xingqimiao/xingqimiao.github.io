<div class="kicker">Chapter 1 · EXECUTIVE SUMMARY</div>
<h2 class="tt" id="chapter-1">Executive Summary</h2>
<div class="lead">The survey recruited respondents through an online questionnaire and describes their self-reported experiences of identity awareness, everyday expression, medical needs, family relationships, campus experiences, employment circumstances, mental health, and social support. The summary below condenses the key findings and directions for action; full data and methods appear in later chapters.</div></div><div class="stats-grid"><div class="stat-card"><div class="num">43.5%</div><div class="lbl">Aged 18–22 · largest age group in the sample</div><div class="sub">Three youngest age groups combined: 73.1% · n=899</div></div><div class="stat-card"><div class="num">16.8%</div><div class="lbl">Can live fully as their gender identity</div><div class="sub">Expression concentrated online: 87.7%</div></div><div class="stat-card"><div class="num">71.9%</div><div class="lbl">Currently on HRT</div><div class="sub">Another 14.6% want to start but have not · n=859</div></div><div class="stat-card"><div class="num">73.7%</div><div class="lbl">At least one negative campus experience</div><div class="sub">Verbal discrimination 49.3% · n=749</div></div><div class="stat-card"><div class="num">88.4%</div><div class="lbl">Frequent emotional distress in the past six months</div><div class="sub">Self-reported: frequent anxiety 61.8% · frequent depressive moods 61.1%</div></div><div class="stat-card"><div class="num">68.2%</div><div class="lbl">History of self-harm</div><div class="sub">Evidence of safety support needs, not identity attribution</div></div></div><blockquote class="warning">
<div class="exec-lead-block"><div class="ld">Important note</div><p style="margin: 0px;">The survey used non-probability sampling, and the sample is dominated by young respondents (43.5% aged 18–22). All results describe only the respondents of this survey and cannot be generalised to transgender and gender-diverse people across China. All "associations" or "correlations" are statistical and do not imply causality.</p></div>
</blockquote><h2 id="1-1-核心发现">1.1 Key Findings</h2><h3 id="样本特征">Sample characteristics</h3><div class="exec-lead-block"><div class="ld">A notably young sample</div><p style="margin: 0px;">Among respondents with a valid age answer (n=899), 43.5% were 18–22, 20.6% were 16–17, and 9.0% were 12–15; the three youngest age groups together accounted for 73.1%. Gender identity was diverse (women 70.3%, non-binary 28.3%, men 11.7%, still exploring 11.6% — multiple choice). Residence was concentrated in urban tiers: tier-1 and new tier-1 cities together 44.0%, tier-2 cities 17.1%, tier-3 and below plus county/town 29.6%, and overseas plus Hong Kong, Macao and Taiwan 9.3%. The medical needs, family dependence, campus experiences, and economic circumstances in this report must be read against this young sample structure.</p></div><h3 id="身份觉察与表达">Identity awareness and expression</h3><div class="exec-lead-block"><div class="ld">Aware early, expression constrained</div><p style="margin: 0px;">More than 80% of respondents (84.8%) noticed their gender identity difference at or before age 17 (13–17: 36.5%; 7–12: 33.9%; before 6: 14.3%). However, only 16.8% could live fully in line with their gender identity; expression is heavily concentrated in online spaces (87.7%) and trusted friend circles (55.3%), while rates in formal settings such as family, school, healthcare, and employment are notably low (all ≤16%). Well-being distributions differ by expression status (Cramér's V=0.144, adjusted p&lt;0.001).</p></div><h3 id="医疗获取与声音需求">Healthcare access and voice needs</h3><div class="exec-lead-block"><div class="ld">HRT common, channels informal, barriers multiple</div><p style="margin: 0px;">71.9% of respondents who answered the HRT-status question (n=859) are currently on HRT, and 14.6% want to start but have not. Among respondents reporting how they obtain HRT (n=716, multiple choice), online informal sellers account for 66.6%, while regular hospital/clinic prescriptions in China account for 29.3%. Medical barriers include family opposition (59.9%), cost burden (51.7%), fear of discrimination (40.0%), resource shortage (35.4%), and insufficient professional supply (33.6%). Those more satisfied with medical care tend to also report higher well-being (rho=0.370, adjusted p&lt;0.001, n=855).</p></div><div class="exec-lead-block"><div class="ld">Monitoring gaps</div><p style="margin: 0px;">Only 20.4% of respondents undergo regular HRT health checks; 28.7% would like to but have no access.</p></div><div class="exec-lead-block"><div class="ld">Widespread voice anxiety</div><p style="margin: 0px;">65.8% of respondents find it difficult or impossible to use a satisfying voice, and 56.9% are dissatisfied with their current voice. Voice is a significant source of gender-expression pressure.</p></div><h3 id="家庭关系">Family</h3><div class="exec-lead-block"><div class="ld">Awareness is not support</div><p style="margin: 0px;">36.6% came out voluntarily; 29.6% said their family does not know; 20.1% were outed passively. Among respondents whose families know (multiple choice), silence/avoidance 58.2%, blame/accusation 48.4%, non-understanding 48.4%, support/acceptance 25.3%. Currently 26.9% describe "completely unsupportive"; among the 312 respondents who directly reviewed family attitude change, 49.4% report improvement, 36.9% report no real change, and 13.8% report deterioration. Those with more family support also tend to report higher well-being (rho=0.321, adjusted p&lt;0.001).</p></div><div class="exec-lead-block"><div class="ld">Family restrictions and violence</div><p style="margin: 0px;">Among respondents answering the family restriction/violence question for the past year (n=474, multiple choice), 39.7% experienced verbal/psychological violence, 33.3% medical interference, 20.0% economic violence, and 14.3% physical violence. Family harm involves medical autonomy, economic control, freedom of movement, and bodily safety.</p></div><h3 id="校园环境">Campus environment</h3><div class="exec-lead-block"><div class="ld">Negative experiences widespread, institutions absent</div><p style="margin: 0px;">73.7% of respondents answering the campus-experience question (n=749, multiple choice) reported at least one clear negative experience: verbal discrimination/ridicule 49.3%, isolation by classmates 44.6%, being required to dress as their assigned sex 36.0%, physical harassment/violence 27.2%. Those reporting more negative campus experiences also tend to report more psychological distress (rho=0.149, adjusted p&lt;0.001).</p></div><p>More than 70% of respondents report almost no support or only sporadic support at school; the share of schools with overall supportive environments is under 20%. School policies that explicitly cover gender diversity and are effectively implemented are extremely rare (5.6%). Those with more school support also tend to report higher well-being (rho=0.278, adjusted p&lt;0.001).</p><h3 id="就业与经济">Employment and economy</h3><div class="exec-lead-block"><div class="ld">Concealment is a primary cost</div><p style="margin: 0px;">Among respondents with work experience (n=267, multiple choice), 52.1% had to hide their identity because it was not disclosed, 36.7% had to maintain an expression inconsistent with their gender identity, 23.2% were questioned because their documents did not match their appearance, and 21.3% were rejected for a job due to gender expression. 50.5% believe their transgender identity negatively affected their financial situation (n=283).</p></div><h3 id="心理健康与安全支持">Mental health and safety support</h3><div class="exec-lead-block"><div class="ld">Distress widespread, pressure multi-layered, support insufficient</div><p style="margin: 0px;">Nearly 90% of respondents (88.4%) experienced at least one high-frequency emotional distress in the past six months (frequent anxiety 61.8%, frequent depressive moods 61.1%, emotional turbulence 58.0%). Pressure sources involve gender identity/body anxiety (83.7%), schoolwork (59.6%), family (58.6%), finances (56.7%), public policy/public opinion (41.4%), and healthcare access (32.9%). 45.5% find it difficult to obtain psychological support.</p></div><div class="exec-lead-block"><div class="ld">Self-harm and suicide-related experiences widespread</div><p style="margin: 0px;">68.2% have a history of self-harm (had it but stopped 47.8%, still engaging in it 20.4%). On suicide-related experiences, lifetime self-reports show ideation without attempt 51.3% and attempt 37.6%; under that question's classification, at least one category was reported by 88.9%. These figures are evidence of safety support needs, not evidence of danger posed by transgender identity, and should not be attributed to the identity itself.</p></div><div class="exec-lead-block"><div class="ld">Well-being skewed low</div><p style="margin: 0px;">47.1% rate their overall well-being negatively (n=896); only 20.7% say they are fairly or very happy.</p></div><h3 id="证件与公共空间">Documents and public space</h3><div class="exec-lead-block"><div class="ld">Document difficulties alongside public services</div><p style="margin: 0px;">Nearly half of relevant respondents (48.8%) encountered questioning or inappropriate treatment in public affairs at least occasionally in the past six months. Those who face more document difficulties also report more difficulties with public services (Cramér's V=0.173, adjusted p=0.013).</p></div><p>Only 14.7% can comfortably use public facilities matching their gender identity; 35.3% are forced to use facilities for their assigned sex; 25.8% try to find alternatives; 22.9% avoid using them whenever possible.</p><div class="exec-lead-block"><div class="ld">Harassment fairly common</div><p style="margin: 0px;">71.3% report some form of harassment experience (n=855). 21.2% frequently experience gender stereotypes or verbal bias; 72.1% at least occasionally (n=900).</p></div><h3 id="社会支持与改变需求">Social support and desire for change</h3><div class="exec-lead-block"><div class="ld">Inner resilience and peer support are the main protective sources</div><p style="margin: 0px;">Their own inner strength 59.9%, friends in the transgender community 49.4%, cisgender friends who understand them 39.4%. Family support ranks low (11.6%). Those with more support sources also tend to report higher well-being (rho=0.179, adjusted p&lt;0.001). 12.7% still say they currently draw strength and support from none.</p></div><div class="exec-lead-block"><div class="ld">Desire for change broad and diverse</div><p style="margin: 0px;">83.7% would like to change body characteristics, but desire for change in voice (68.3%), finances (65.3%), mental health (63.5%), legal documents (55.6%), and family relationships (50.2%) is equally common — all above half. 80.0% express a strong desire for change (strongly want 43.5% + somewhat want 36.5%).</p></div><h2 id="1-2-跨章节主线">1.2 Cross-chapter threads</h2><div class="theme-strip"><div class="row"><div class="theme-block"><div class="no">01</div><div class="tx"><strong>A young and diverse sample, aware early, but severely constrained in everyday expression</strong>: formal settings (family, school, healthcare, employment) lack safe conditions for expression.
</div></div><div class="theme-block"><div class="no">02</div><div class="tx"><strong>Healthcare, family, campus, employment, documents and public space form interwoven practical barriers</strong>: for example, family opposition is reported alongside barriers to healthcare access, economic support, and identity expression; document-mismatch difficulties also appear across employment, public affairs, and public facilities.
</div></div><div class="theme-block"><div class="no">03</div><div class="tx"><strong>Psychological distress must be interpreted within multi-layered external pressures, not attributed to identity itself</strong>: reported pressure sources involve body, schoolwork, family, finances, and institutional environments; distress should be read alongside multi-layered pressure, insufficient support, and safety needs — and cannot establish causal direction.
</div></div><div class="theme-block"><div class="no">04</div><div class="tx"><strong>Inner resilience and relational networks protect, but coverage is uneven</strong>: structural responsibility should not be shifted onto individual resilience; services must actively reach the groups with the least support.
</div></div><div class="theme-block"><div class="no">05</div><div class="tx"><strong>Desire for change spans body, voice, finances, psychology, law, and social environment</strong>: this is not a single medical issue.
</div></div></div></div><h2 id="1-3-行动方向">1.3 Directions for action</h2><p>The directions below draw on the survey findings and are intended as reference for service providers, policy discussions, and community organisations — not finalized policies that have passed cost-benefit or feasibility review:</p><h3 id="医疗服务">Medical services</h3><ul>
<li>Expand regular, affordable HRT assessment, prescription and follow-up services; integrate HRT health monitoring into the service chain.</li>
<li>Provide harm-reduction information during the transition period where formal services do not yet sufficiently cover.</li>
<li>Treat voice support as a standalone service: offer informed-consent, non-conversion-based training and referral.</li>
<li>Improve privacy protection and professional training in medical services.</li>
<li>Review potential accessibility barriers related to age and guardian-consent requirements, and explore institutional paths that balance individual autonomy, informed consent, and minor protection.</li>
</ul><h3 id="家庭支持">Family support</h3><ul>
<li>Provide long-term family education and support programmes that can be entered repeatedly.</li>
<li>Offer families accurate, non-pathologizing information that addresses misconceptions, traditional values, and social pressure.</li>
<li>Build low-threshold safety plans, legal and psychological referrals, and emergency economic support.</li>
</ul><h3 id="教育环境">Schools</h3><ul>
<li>Explicitly include protection of gender identity and expression in anti-bullying policies.</li>
<li>Establish support mechanisms with accountable staff, confidential procedures, and complaint channels.</li>
<li>Provide professional training for teachers.</li>
<li>Gradually reduce social stereotypes through systematic gender education, and promote gender education for students and families.</li>
</ul><h3 id="就业与经济">Employment and economy</h3><ul>
<li>Drive inclusive redesign of recruitment, dress codes, document verification, name use, and anti-harassment processes.</li>
<li>Offer more flexible, privacy-respecting alternatives in identity-verification processes.</li>
</ul><h3 id="心理健康与安全支持">Mental health and safety support</h3><ul>
<li>Provide trauma-informed, non-pathologizing psychological services covering a range of distress, not a single diagnosis.</li>
<li>Build low-threshold crisis support and long-term psychological services.</li>
<li>In presentations of self-harm and suicide-related content, connect directly to support resources and handle the topic neutrally, without sensationalism.</li>
</ul><h3 id="证件与公共服务">Documents and public services</h3><ul>
<li>Simplify document-change procedures and improve accessibility; reform front-desk identity-verification processes.</li>
<li>Increase the supply of gender-neutral public facilities and push for gender-neutral redesign.</li>
</ul><h3 id="社会支持与资源可见性">Social support and resource visibility</h3><ul>
<li>Expand peer networks, partner and family support, while respecting individual resilience without transferring responsibility onto individuals.</li>
<li>Make local support resources more visible and expand actual supply.</li>
<li>Proactively reach the groups with the least support.</li>
</ul><h3 id="跨领域原则">Cross-cutting principles</h3><ul>
<li>Design support around diverse needs for change, covering body, voice, finances, psychology, law, and social environment.</li>
<li>Respect individual autonomy: respond to desire for change while respecting those who are unsure or do not want to change.</li>
</ul><h2 id="1-4-研究局限">1.4 Research limitations</h2><ul>
<li><strong>Non-probability sampling and a young sample restrict generalisation</strong>: the results cannot be generalised to all transgender and gender-diverse people in China.</li>
<li><strong>Self-report and cross-sectional design limit interpretation</strong>: all data are self-reported and collected at a single point in time; associations are not used to infer temporal order or causality.</li>
<li><strong>An unexamined item is not "no association"; an item short of adjusted significance is not "no relationship"</strong>: some pre-specified tests may fall short due to sample size, measurement, or analysis limits.</li>
<li><strong>Passing technical QA does not establish representativeness, validity, external validity, or scientific conclusions</strong>: technical QA only verifies the accuracy of numeric processing.</li>
<li><strong>Reporting bias on sensitive topics</strong>: domestic violence, self-harm, and suicide content may be under- or over-reported.</li>
<li><strong>Measurement limits of single items or multi-select proxies</strong>: some core concepts cannot capture all dimensions.</li>
</ul><p>Full research limitations and methods are in Chapters 2 and 11.</p><h2 id="1-5-如何阅读本报告">1.5 How to read this report</h2><ul>
<li>This report has 12 chapters: Chapter 2 introduces methods and limitations; Chapters 3–10 present findings by domain; Chapter 11 synthesizes conclusions and directions for action; Chapter 12 provides the appendix.</li>
<li>Results already assigned a Result_ID can be checked against the public tracking table described in Appendix C for aggregate figures and calculation notes; FIG028–FIG033 are registered in Appendix A by Figure_ID, with their aggregation recomputation records currently retained in internal audit materials. None of the public materials can be used to trace individual responses.</li>
<li>This report recommends keeping three boundaries in mind: within-sample description, self-reported data, and statistical association.</li>
<li>Sensitive content (domestic violence, self-harm, suicide) is presented neutrally, without sensationalism or pathologization; relevant chapters provide psychological support and crisis-intervention resources (Chapters 8 and Appendix E).</li>
</ul><div class="exec-lead-block"><div class="ld">Acknowledgements</div><p style="margin: 0px;">We thank every respondent who shared their real experiences in the questionnaire. This report rests on respondents' self-reports and provides an empirical starting point for improving the situation of transgender and gender-diverse people. Improvement requires systemic collaboration across healthcare, family, education, employment, law, and social environment — and respect for each individual's autonomy and diverse needs.</p></div><hr></div>

<h2 class="opener-title" id="chapter-2">Background, Sample Characteristics, and Methodological Boundaries</h2>
<p class="opener-summary">Barriers faced by transgender and gender-diverse people in healthcare access, family support, school environments, employment conditions, and public services have been widely documented in international literature. A cross-sectional study based on the Chengdu TREASURE cohort also reports a statistical association between traumatic experiences (including LGBTQ+-related adversity) and depressive symptoms, discussed within a framework of minority stress, social support, and coping (Yang et al., 2026; see Appendix F).</p>

</div>
<div class="opener-chips"><span class="opener-chip">2.1 Background and purpose</span><span class="opener-chip">2.2 Sample characteristics</span><span class="opener-chip">2.3 Methods and research limitations</span><span class="opener-chip">2.4 Terminology</span><span class="opener-chip">2.5 Structure of the report</span></div>
</div><h2 id="2-1-研究背景与目的">2.1 Background and purpose</h2><p>This study uses a cross-sectional self-report questionnaire design and recruited respondents through online platforms. The survey covers age of identity awareness, expression settings, use and access to hormone replacement therapy (HRT), family awareness and support, campus and workplace experiences, mental health status, difficulties using public services and facilities, sources of social support, and willingness to change the status quo. The survey makes no clinical diagnosis; prior diagnosis experiences reported by respondents are self-reports, unverified against medical records. Other experiences are likewise unverified by physiological measurement or third-party checks.</p><h2 id="2-2-样本特征">2.2 Sample characteristics</h2><h3 id="2-2-1-年龄分布-样本明显偏年轻">2.2.1 Age distribution: a notably young sample</h3><div class="finding-with-first-paragraph"><div class="finding-lead finding-lead--finding"><span class="ft">The sample is notably young; 18–22 is the largest age group</span><span class="chip">Key finding</span></div><p>Among respondents who answered the age question (n=899), the distribution shows a clear youth bias.</p></div><div class="figure-block"><figure class="report-figure" id="FIG001"><img src="/pic/report/2026-transgender-life-survey-reader-edition/FIG001.en.png" alt="FIG001: Age group distribution of respondents"></figure></div><p>18–22 accounted for 43.5% (391 people), the largest age group; 16–17 for 20.6% (185 people); 12–15 for 9.0% (81 people). The three youngest age groups together accounted for 73.1% of respondents with a valid answer. 23–29 accounted for 23.2% (209 people), 30–39 for 3.4% (31 people), and the remaining low-frequency categories together 0.2% (2 people).</p><p>This age structure is directly related to the recruitment channels (mainly online social platforms and community networks) and the sample type (non-probability convenience sampling). The high share of young respondents means that the medical needs, family dependence, campus experiences, and economic circumstances described here must be read against this age backdrop. Topics in later chapters — family conflict, enrolment status, minors' medical decision rights, and economic autonomy — are closely tied to this age structure.</p><blockquote>
<p><strong>Reading note</strong>: the findings describe only the respondents of this survey and cannot be directly generalised to all transgender and gender-diverse people across China. All percentages use valid respondents to that question as the denominator; the number of valid respondents may differ between questions.</p>
</blockquote><h3 id="2-2-2-性别认同的多样性">2.2.2 Diversity of gender identities</h3><div class="finding-with-first-paragraph"><div class="finding-lead finding-lead--finding"><span class="ft">Gender identities in the sample are diverse</span><span class="chip">Key finding</span></div><p>Among valid respondents to the gender-identity question (n=899, multi-select), women accounted for 70.3% (632 people), non-binary 28.3% (254 people), men 11.7% (105 people), still exploring 11.6% (104 people), genderfluid 10.1% (91 people), and agender 7.7% (69 people). Because this was a multi-select question, some respondents selected more than one category, so the percentages sum to more than 100%.</p></div><p>This distribution indicates clear diversity in gender identity among respondents: they should not be reduced to a single "transgender" category. When reading the medical needs, expression strategies, and support sources in later chapters, keep in mind that the experiences of people with different gender identities may differ. Submission dates recoverable from the current workbook run from 2 June to 30 July 2026 (workbook local time; the project materials do not save a separate timezone note). The questionnaire contains closed-ended choice questions (single and multi-select) and ordered rating items, covering identity awareness, expression, healthcare, family, campus, employment, mental health, public services, and social support. The design drew on common frameworks of international transgender health surveys, with some wording and options adapted to the Chinese context.</p><p>Recruitment channels included transgender community social media accounts, online mutual-aid groups, social platforms of relevant non-profit organisations, and snowball forwarding by respondents. Participation was voluntary: the current questionnaire states the purpose, data-use approach, and privacy protections at the outset and requires confirmation of informed consent before proceeding. The project's surviving materials do not record formal inclusion criteria for recruits, minimum-age statements, or processes for minor respondents' own informed participation and guardian consent (or waiver), so this report cannot retroactively assume those processes; the age question does include a 12–15 option, and the sample includes respondents under 18. This material gap is listed as a research ethics and interpretation limitation.</p><h3 id="2-3-2-数据处理">2.3.2 Data processing</h3><p>Raw questionnaire data were processed as follows:</p><div class="steps-grid"><div class="step"><div class="idx">1</div><div><div class="tt">Consent eligibility screening</div><div class="tx">4 submissions with explicit refusal and no subsequent valid answers were excluded. Association tests use chi-square tests (categorical variables) or Spearman rank correlation (ordinal variables), reporting effect sizes (Cramér's V or Spearman's rho) and Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-values. All statistical tests are exploratory; adjusted p&lt;0.05 is treated as statistically significant.</p><p>"Association" or "correlation" in this report means statistical association only, not causation. The cross-sectional design cannot establish temporal order or causal direction. Mental health, well-being, and support analyses are all based on self-report. The survey makes no clinical diagnosis; the prior-diagnosis question likewise records only self-reports, unverified against medical records. The psychological-status items are not a single validated clinical scale.</p><h3 id="2-3-4-研究局限">2.3.4 Research limitations</h3><div class="warning"><svg class="ic" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="currentColor" aria-hidden="true"><path d="M12 2 1 21h22L12 2zm1 14h-2v2h2v-2zm0-7h-2v5h2V9z"></path></svg><div><div class="tt">Non-probability sampling</div><div class="tx">Respondents were recruited voluntarily through online channels, not random sampling, so representativeness cannot be guaranteed. The results describe only the respondents of this survey and cannot be generalised to all transgender and gender-diverse people in China or to other countries and regions.
</div></div></div><p>The study has the following important limitations, which readers should consider when interpreting results:</p><div class="limit-grid"><div class="limit-card"><svg class="ic" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="currentColor" aria-hidden="true"><path d="M12 2 1 21h22L12 2zm1 14h-2v2h2v-2zm0-7h-2v5h2V9z"></path></svg><div><div class="tt">Young sample age structure</div><div class="tx">As noted in 2.2.1, respondents aged 18–22 hold the largest share (43.5%), while those 23 and above together account for 26.6% (23–29: 23.2%; 30–39: 3.4%). This age structure shapes the reading of medical needs, family dependence, campus experiences, employment circumstances, and financial conditions: younger respondents may more often be in school, dependent on family financial support, not yet in the labour market, or still in early stages of family conflict.
</div></div></div><div class="limit-card"><svg class="ic" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="currentColor" aria-hidden="true"><path d="M12 2 1 21h22L12 2zm1 14h-2v2h2v-2zm0-7h-2v5h2V9z"></path></svg><div><div class="tt">Self-reported data</div><div class="tx">All data are self-reported by respondents and unverified by third parties. Descriptions of emotional distress and mental health are based on subjective experience and are not clinical diagnoses. Reports of family reactions, campus experiences, and workplace discrimination reflect respondents' perception and memory, which may be affected by recall bias and personal interpretation.
</div></div></div><div class="limit-card"><svg class="ic" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="currentColor" aria-hidden="true"><path d="M12 2 1 21h22L12 2zm1 14h-2v2h2v-2zm0-7h-2v5h2V9z"></path></svg><div><div class="tt">Cross-sectional design</div><div class="tx">All data were collected at a single point in time, so temporal order or causality between variables cannot be established. For example, the statistical association between identity expression and well-being cannot determine whether expression affects well-being, whether well-being affects willingness to express, or whether both are influenced by third factors (e.g. family support, financial conditions).
</div></div></div><div class="limit-card"><svg class="ic" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="currentColor" aria-hidden="true"><path d="M12 2 1 21h22L12 2zm1 14h-2v2h2v-2zm0-7h-2v5h2V9z"></path></svg><div><div class="tt">Measurement instruments</div><div class="tx">Some concepts in the questionnaire (e.g. "family support", "school support", "well-being") are measured with single-item ordinal ratings or multi-select proxies that cannot capture every dimension of the concept. For instance, "family support" is measured by one rating item about overall feelings, without distinguishing emotional support, financial support, medical-decision support, and other layers.
</div></div></div><div class="limit-card"><svg class="ic" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="currentColor" aria-hidden="true"><path d="M12 2 1 21h22L12 2zm1 14h-2v2h2v-2zm0-7h-2v5h2V9z"></path></svg><div><div class="tt">Limits of interpretation of statistical associations</div><div class="tx">All "associations" or "correlations" passing statistical tests in this report are exploratory results, not preceded by sample-size calculations or effect-size estimation. Tests that fell short of adjusted significance (e.g. F024, F025, F026) cannot be read as "no association" — they may be limited by sample size, measurement instruments, or analytical methods.
</div></div></div><div class="limit-card"><svg class="ic" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="currentColor" aria-hidden="true"><path d="M12 2 1 21h22L12 2zm1 14h-2v2h2v-2zm0-7h-2v5h2V9z"></path></svg><div><div class="tt">Reporting bias on sensitive topics</div><div class="tx">Some questions touch on highly sensitive content such as domestic violence, self-harm, and suicide-related experiences. </div></div></div><div class="limit-card"><svg class="ic" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="currentColor" aria-hidden="true"><path d="M12 2 1 21h22L12 2zm1 14h-2v2h2v-2zm0-7h-2v5h2V9z"></path></svg><div><div class="tt">Limited historical comparison</div><div class="tx">The report's structure was informed by the 2021 national transgender health survey, but differences in questionnaire wording, recruitment channels, sample composition, and statistical methods mean the two surveys are not strictly comparable. </div></div></div></div><h3 id="2-3-5-伦理与隐私">2.3.5 Ethics and privacy</h3><p>The survey followed these principles in design and implementation:</p><ul>

</ul><h2 id="2-4-术语说明">2.4 Terminology</h2><p>For ease of reading, the report defines the following core terms:</p><ul>
<li><strong>Transgender</strong>: people whose gender identity differs from the sex assigned at birth. In this report "transgender" is used broadly, covering trans women, trans men, and other people whose gender identity does not fit the binary framework.</li>
<li><strong>Gender diverse</strong>: including but not limited to transgender, non-binary, genderfluid, agender, and other gender identities.</li>
<li><strong>Sex assigned at birth</strong>: the sex assigned at birth based on physical characteristics, usually "male" or "female".</li>
<li><strong>Gender identity</strong>: an individual's inner sense and experience of their own gender.</li>
<li><strong>Gender expression</strong>: external presentation of gender through clothing, behaviour, manner, voice, and the like.</li>
<li><strong>Hormone Replacement Therapy (HRT)</strong>: medical measures using hormonal medication to move secondary sex characteristics toward the recognized gender. In this report "HRT" covers feminizing HRT (oestrogen and anti-androgens) and masculinizing HRT (testosterone).</li>
<li><strong>Gender affirmation</strong>: the umbrella of medical, social, and legal measures supporting an individual to live in line with their gender identity, including but not limited to HRT, surgery, document changes, and social support.</li>
<li><strong>Coming out</strong>: voluntarily disclosing one's transgender identity to others. This report distinguishes "voluntary coming out" from "passive coming out / passive discovery".</li>
<li><strong>Cisgender</strong>: people whose gender identity matches the sex assigned at birth.</li>

</blockquote><h2 id="3-3-身份公开的方式与范围">3.3 How and how widely identity is disclosed</h2><h3 id="3-3-1-身份公开的层次性">3.3.1 The layered nature of disclosure</h3><div class="finding-with-first-paragraph"><div class="finding-lead finding-lead--finding"><span class="ft">Disclosure mostly happens within a few trusted relationships and online spaces</span><span class="chip">Key finding</span></div><p>Among respondents who answered the disclosure-audience question (n=900, multi-select), disclosure is clearly stratified. Telling a few trusted friends 62.8% (565 people), only online 39.6% (356 people), telling family (voluntary coming out) 32.9% (296 people). By contrast, disclosure to wider groups is rarer: passively exposed due to physical changes or other reasons ("blown out of the closet") 11.1% (100 people), fully hidden 9.0% (81 people), disclosed at school/workplace 7.7% (69 people), and fully open 6.8% (61 people).</p></div><p>This distribution shows that disclosure is not a binary "out or not" state but a contextual, layered process. Most respondents selectively disclose to trusted individuals or small groups rather than to all social relationships. The high online share matches F004 and underscores the role of online space in expression and community connection.</p><h3 id="3-3-2-被动暴露与持续隐藏">3.3.2 Passive exposure and continued concealment</h3><div class="finding-with-first-paragraph"><div class="finding-lead finding-lead--finding"><span class="ft">Passive exposure and full concealment are both real identity-management experiences</span><span class="chip">Key finding</span></div><p>Besides voluntary disclosure, some respondents experienced passive exposure or chose continued concealment. In the disclosure-audience question (n=900, multi-select), 9.0% (81 people) chose "not disclosed to anyone at all" and 11.1% (100 people) reported passive exposure ("blown out of the closet") due to physical changes or other reasons; 5.7% (51 people) do not voluntarily disclose at school/workplace but may be detected. In the supplementary passive-exposure options, 4.3% (39 people) reported passive exposure.</p></div><p><strong>Possible mechanisms / interpretive discussion (not directly measured by this question)</strong>: passive exposure (having one's identity disclosed by others without consent) can bring safety risks, broken relationships, or forced encounters with unprepared reactions. Continued concealment may carry long-term psychological costs, limited expression, and the strain of maintaining a double identity in social interaction. These mechanisms draw on community experience and existing research; they cannot be treated as direct measurements from this question.</p><blockquote>
<p><strong>Reading note</strong>: the percentages above come from different question versions and options and cannot be directly summed or compared. "Passive exposure" and "continued concealment" may co-occur in the same person (e.g. exposed once, then worked to re-conceal).</p>
</blockquote><h2 id="3-4-身份表达与其他经历的关联">3.4 Identity expression and other experiences</h2><h3 id="3-4-1-身份表达与校园负面经历">3.4.1 Expression and negative campus experiences</h3><div class="finding-with-first-paragraph"><div class="finding-lead finding-lead--finding"><span class="ft">The pre-specified test between expression and negative campus experiences fell short of adjusted significance</span><span class="chip">Key finding</span></div><p>This analysis found no clear statistical association between identity-expression status and negative campus experiences (Cramér's V=0.108, adjusted p=0.118, n=729).</p></div><p>This does not mean expression and campus experience are unrelated. Expression is recorded with a single five-level item, and campus negative experiences are compressed into a simplified indicator, which may miss specific settings, frequency, and severity. These data can only show that evidence is not yet clear-cut; they cannot prove "no relationship".</p><p>This finding also implies that even among respondents able to express their gender identity fully, negative campus experiences may still occur for other reasons — expression not matching stereotypes, being seen as "not masculine/feminine enough", or simply because of transgender identity itself. Campus support must cover all gender-diverse students, not only those "unable to express".</p><h3 id="3-4-2-性吸引对象与探索">3.4.2 Attraction and exploration</h3><div class="finding-with-first-paragraph"><div class="finding-lead finding-lead--finding"><span class="ft">Attraction is diverse; pansexual and femininity-oriented attraction are relatively common</span><span class="chip">Key finding</span></div><p>Among respondents who answered the attraction question (n=878 <!-- calc: N(R149) -->, multi-select), women/femininity 49.2% (432 people), everyone (pansexual) 34.2% (300 people), men/masculinity 27.4% (241 people), non-binary/genderfluid people 24.5% (215 people), still exploring 21.6% (190 people), and attracted to no gender (asexual) 8.5% (75 people). <!-- calc: R149 --> <!-- calc: R150 --> <!-- calc: R151 --> <!-- calc: R152 --> <!-- calc: R153 --> <!-- calc: R154 --> <!-- cross: 未绑定Finding_ID的MASTER描述性补充 --></p></div><p>This distribution indicates attraction is not concentrated on a single gender expression; a fair share report pansexual (33.8% <!-- calc: R150 -->) or still exploring (21.7% <!-- calc: R153 -->). This diversity suggests support services and community discussions should not assume a single attraction pattern — identity expression and intimate relationships are likewise diverse.</p><h2 id="3-5-本章小结">3.5 Chapter summary</h2><p>The findings in this chapter sketch a complex picture of identity awareness, disclosure, and expression:</p><ol class="chapter-summary" style="list-style: none; padding-left: 0px;">
<li><p><strong>Early awareness</strong>: more than 80% of respondents noticed their gender identity difference at or before age 17; family and school reactions during this period are crucial for individual development.</p>
</li>
<li><p><strong>Constrained expression</strong>: the share living fully as their gender identity is not high (16.8%), and over 60% face clear setting-based limits. Well-being distributions differ by expression status — a link, not proof of causation.</p>
</li>
<li><p><strong>Setting divergence</strong>: online spaces and trusted friend circles are the main settings for expression (87.7% and 55.3%), while formal settings — family, school, healthcare, employment — are clearly lower (all ≤16%).</p>
</li>
<li><p><strong>Layered disclosure</strong>: disclosure is stratified; most respondents selectively disclose to close friends, online communities, and a few family members rather than to all social relationships.</p>
</li>
<li><p><strong>Privacy risks</strong>: passive exposure and continued concealment are both real identity-management experiences; support for privacy protection and safe expression must cover voluntary disclosure, passive exposure, and continued concealment alike.</p>
</li>
</ol><p>These findings suggest that supporting gender-diverse individuals' expression and disclosure requires more than "how to come out" information: it requires improving safety and inclusivity in formal settings — family, school, healthcare, workplace — while respecting each person's autonomy over the scope, timing, and manner of disclosure.</p><p>Later chapters present experiences in healthcare, family, campus, employment, and mental health, all of which are closely tied to the conditions and costs of identity expression.</p><hr></div>

</blockquote><h2 id="4-5-声音需求与满意度">4.5 Voice needs and satisfaction</h2><div class="finding-with-first-paragraph"><div class="finding-lead finding-lead--finding"><span class="ft">Voice is a prominent source of gender-expression pressure; satisfaction is generally low</span><span class="chip">Key finding</span></div><p><strong>Difficulty using one's voice</strong>: among respondents who answered the voice-use question (n=798), voice causes significant anxiety for many.</p></div><div class="figure-block"><figure class="report-figure" id="FIG009"><img src="/pic/report/2026-transgender-life-survey-reader-edition/FIG009.en.png" alt="FIG009: Difficulty using one's voice"></figure></div><p>"Very hard; my voice is one of my main anxiety sources" 46.2% (369 people), and "impossible" 19.5% (156 people). </blockquote><h2 id="4-6-本次数据没有显示明确关联的检验">4.6 Tests with no clear association in these data</h2><div class="finding-with-first-paragraph"><div class="finding-lead finding-lead--finding"><span class="ft">The unmet-HRT-need vs. distress association fell short of adjusted significance</span><span class="chip">Key finding</span></div><p>This analysis found no clear statistical association between "want to use HRT but not yet started" and psychological distress (Spearman rho=-0.070, n=822, adjusted p=0.087).</p></div><p>This does not mean HRT accessibility is unrelated to mental health. <li><p><strong>Concealment is the main cost</strong>: 52.1% must conceal their identity because it is not disclosed; 36.7% must maintain expression inconsistent with their gender identity. Sustained concealment and inconsistent expression are themselves the most common workplace burdens.</p>

</li>
<li><p><strong>Sample limits</strong>: with a young sample, those with work experience (n=267) are a small share, so findings mainly reflect job-hunting/work-experienced respondents, not the full sample or the broader working population.</p>
</li>
</ol><p>Improving transgender employment environments requires: explicit anti-discrimination policies and complaint mechanisms; inclusive redesign of recruitment, dress, name use, and document-verification processes; and support services for the psychological costs of concealment and inconsistent expression. Beyond overt discrimination, sustained concealment and inconsistent expression are themselves significant burdens in the workplace and need to be visible in policy design and service provision.</p><hr></div>

<li><p><strong>Public facility use</strong>: only 14.7% can comfortably use identity-matching public facilities. Avoidance (22.9%) and alternatives (25.8%) are common coping strategies, indicating demand for gender-neutral public facilities.</p>
</li>
<li><p><strong>Widespread harassment</strong>: 71.3% report some form of harassment; offline verbal (26.1%) and online harassment/cyberbullying (25.5%) are most common. Sexual harassment/sexual violence (18.8%) and physical violence (6.1%) are also reported.</p>
</li>
<li><p><strong>Persistent everyday bias</strong>: 21.2% frequently experience gender stereotypes or verbal bias; more than 70% (72.1%) have such experiences. Low-intensity, repetitive bias needs to be included in service design.</p>
</li>
<li><p><strong>Resource visibility gaps</strong>: over 80% (83.4%) know very few local transgender support resources (0–2), indicating room to improve both supply and visibility.</p>
</li>
</ol><p>Public-life barriers thus involve three layers: document systems, public facility design, and the social environment. Improvement paths include: simplified, accessible document change; reformed front-desk identity-verification processes; gender-neutral public facility supply; and inclusive education for society.</p><p>Chapter 10 presents respondents' social support sources, resilience resources, and desire for change.</p><hr></div>

</div><h2 id="11-1-跨章节核心主线">11.1 Cross-chapter core threads</h2><div class="theme-strip ch11-overview"><div class="strip-title"><span class="t">Cross-chapter core threads</span></div><div class="row"><div class="theme-block"><div class="no">01</div><div class="tx"><strong>Young and diverse sample, early awareness, but severely constrained everyday expression</strong></div></div><div class="theme-block"><div class="no">02</div><div class="tx"><strong>Interwoven real barriers across healthcare, family, campus, employment, documents, and public space</strong></div></div><div class="theme-block"><div class="no">03</div><div class="tx"><strong>Distress must be interpreted within multi-layered external pressures, not attributed to identity itself</strong></div></div><div class="theme-block"><div class="no">04</div><div class="tx"><strong>Inner resilience and relational networks protect, but coverage is uneven</strong></div></div><div class="theme-block"><div class="no">05</div><div class="tx"><strong>Desire for change spans body, voice, finances, psychology, law, and social environment</strong></div></div></div></div><p>Combining findings from Chapters 2 to 10, the survey presents five interwoven threads:</p><h3 id="主线一-年轻且多元的样本-较早觉察身份-但日常表达高度受限">Thread 1: Young and diverse sample, early awareness, severely constrained everyday expression</h3><p>The sample is dominated by respondents aged 18–22 (43.5%), and gender identities are diverse. More than 80% began noticing their gender identity difference at or before age 17. Yet the share able to live fully as their gender identity is not high (16.8%); expression is heavily concentrated online (87.7%) and in trusted friend circles (55.3%), while formal settings — family, school, healthcare, employment — are clearly lower.</p><p><strong>Implication</strong>: gender awareness emerges in a life stage where family and school still heavily shape daily life, but formal settings lack conditions for safe expression. Support systems need both age-appropriateness and setting-specific targeting.</p><h3 id="主线二-医疗-家庭-校园-就业-证件与公共空间构成相互交织的现实障碍">Thread 2: Interwoven real barriers across healthcare, family, campus, employment, documents, and public space</h3><ul>
<li><strong>Healthcare</strong>: HRT access relies heavily on informal channels (online private sellers 66.6%); barriers include family opposition (59.9%), cost (51.7%), discrimination concerns, and resource shortage.</li>
<li><strong>Family</strong>: awareness is not support; silence, blame, and non-understanding are common; some respondents experience verbal, medical, economic, and personal restrictions.</li>
<li><strong>Campus</strong>: 73.7% report negative campus experiences; school policies explicitly covering gender diversity are extremely rare (5.6%).</li>
<li><strong>Employment</strong>: concealment (52.1%) and maintaining inconsistent expression (36.7%) are the main costs; 50.5% believe their transgender identity negatively affected their finances.</li>
<li><strong>Public space</strong>: only 14.7% can comfortably use identity-matching public facilities; document difficulties accompany public-service difficulties.</li>
</ul><p><strong>Implication</strong>: these barriers are not isolated; they are reported across domains at once. For example, family opposition appears alongside healthcare access, economic support, and identity-expression barriers; document-mismatch difficulties likewise appear across employment, public affairs, and public facilities. Action requires cross-departmental, systemic collaboration.</p><h3 id="主线三-心理困扰必须放在多层外部压力中解释-而非归因于身份本身">Thread 3: Distress must be interpreted within multi-layered external pressures, not attributed to identity itself</h3><p>Nearly 90% experienced at least one high-frequency distress in the past six months; pressure sources include gender identity/body anxiety (83.7%), schoolwork (59.6%), family (58.6%), finances (56.7%), public policy/social opinion (41.4%), and medical access (32.9%). Self-harm (68.2%) and suicide-related experiences (88.9%) are common in this sample, and 45.5% find psychological support hard to access.</p><p><strong>Implication</strong>: distress runs parallel to external environmental pressure, insufficient support, and safety needs; cross-sectional data cannot confirm causal direction, and these experiences are not evidence of transgender-identity danger. Mental health support should be trauma-informed and non-pathologizing, responding at once to family, educational, economic, and medical barriers.</p><h3 id="主线四-内在韧性与关系网络提供保护-但覆盖并不均衡">Thread 4: Inner resilience and relational networks protect, but coverage is uneven</h3><p>The main protective sources are personal inner strength (59.9%) and transgender-community friends (49.4%), while family support is limited in coverage (11.6%). Diverse support sources correlate positively with well-being, but 12.7% still report no current strength or support.</p><p><strong>Implication</strong>: protective factors include personal resources as well as peer, partner, and community connections. However, structural responsibility should not be shifted onto individual resilience — uneven network coverage means services must proactively reach groups in extreme support scarcity.</p><h3 id="主线五-改变需求横跨身体-声音-经济-心理-法律与社会环境">Thread 5: Desire for change spans body, voice, finances, psychology, law, and social environment</h3><p>83.7% want body changes, but desire for voice (68.3%), finances (65.3%), mental health (63.5%), legal documents (55.6%), and family relationships (50.2%) is equally common. 80.0% express a strong desire for change.</p><p><strong>Implication</strong>: the need for change is not one medical issue but a combination of body, financial, psychological, legal, and social-environmental dimensions. Action recommendations should respond to this diversity.</p><h2 id="11-2-行动方向">11.2 Directions for action</h2><div class="action-grid"><div class="action-block"><div class="ab-t">11.2.1 Medical
