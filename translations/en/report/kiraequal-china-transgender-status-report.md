### I. Executive Summary

Based on several quantitative surveys and qualitative interviews conducted between 2019 and 2025, this report reviews the living conditions of transgender people in mainland China. The data show that although public discussion of gender diversity has grown, transgender people still face systemic barriers in core areas such as healthcare, family, education, and employment. Strict medical thresholds (especially the "parental consent" requirement applied even to adults) push large numbers of people toward highly risky, unregulated channels for hormones. More than 70% of transgender minors and young people experience rejection or even violence at home and on campus, which drives extreme dropout rates and a mental health crisis. While the internet provides a valuable mutual-aid network for the community, the severe shortage of offline resources and tightening of public space leave the group's living situation highly hidden and fragile. Behind the huge statistics are ordinary lives trying to find truth and dignity in the cracks.

### III. Main Body

#### 1. Scope and methodology

The analysis in this report is strictly limited to four core sources: an international survey, "I Need My Parents' Permission to Be Myself" (2019); the Beijing Tongyu Culture 2021 National Transgender Health Survey (2021); the Briefing Paper on the Living Conditions of Transgender Communities in Mainland China, Hong Kong, Macao, and Taiwan (2025); and MtF.Report's Interim Report on a Broad Survey of Transgender People (2025). The surveys differ in wording and sample; this report presents them side by side and does not force conflicting figures into one. We thank all the contributors and everyone who helped.

#### 2. Overall situation: age structure and the mental health crisis

**Problem**: transgender people show a clear trend toward earlier self-awareness, but it comes with extremely high mental health risks.
**Evidence**: the youthfulness of the group recurs throughout the sources. [Beijing Tongyu Culture / 2021] notes that most transgender people begin to notice their gender identity before age 18; the broad survey in [MtF.Report / 2025] shows that adolescents born 2000–2010 make up the vast majority of the sample, with the 12–16 group growing notably. On mental health, [MtF.Report / 2025] shows up to 72% of respondents self-report depression, and 81.7% have had suicidal ideation or attempts.

![Psychological problems1](/pic/report/kiraequal-china-transgender-status-report-01.en.jpeg)

**Analysis**: the younger generation encounters the concept of gender identity online earlier, and therefore establishes self-awareness earlier. However, support systems are far from keeping pace with their awareness.
**Impact**: this disconnect between self-knowledge and reality leaves many teenagers isolated and helpless — over 60% of respondents have engaged in self-harm [MtF.Report / 2025].
**Summary**: for some transgender teenagers, the earlier they recognize who they really are, the earlier they begin to face enormous survival pressure alone.

#### 3. Access to healthcare and bodily autonomy

**Problem**: the strict thresholds of current medical regulations effectively deprive many transgender people of bodily autonomy.
**Evidence**: under the Technical Management Specification for Gender Reassignment Technology (2022 edition), gender-confirming surgery requires the applicant to be at least 18, unmarried, and without a criminal record — and, most critically, **to have informed consent from immediate family members**. On hormone replacement therapy (HRT), [MtF.Report / 2025] shows that 80.5% of respondents are on HRT, but as many as **77.1%** obtain their hormones through online resellers or informal channel, and only 21.1% through a regular hospital.

![HRT80](/pic/report/kiraequal-china-transgender-status-report-02.en.png)

**Analysis**: although the law holds 18-year-olds to be fully competent adults, medical practice still demands "parental consent". This lacks any medical rationale and traps adults whose families do not accept them. The absence of official HRT guidelines means extremely scarce formal medical resources.
**Impact**: to relieve the intense anxiety of gender incongruence, many must buy medication through "grey channels" and self-medicate — becoming so-called 药娘, trans women on unmonitored hormones. This carries huge risks of counterfeit drugs and liver damage; some desperate individuals even attempt extremely dangerous self-performed surgery [international survey / 2019].

![GtKg7PUbMAAAk8d](/pic/report/kiraequal-china-transgender-status-report-03.jpg)

**Summary**: amid institutional gaps and barriers, a transgender person's transition often becomes a lonely adventure that runs without a safety net.

#### 4. Family and the plight of minors

**Problem**: for transgender minors and young people, family is often not a safe harbor but the harshest source of pressure.
**Evidence**: surveys show that nearly 90% of birth families cannot fully accept their transgender child [briefing / 2025]. [MtF.Report / 2025] reveals a cruel pattern: 70.4% of respondents were passively "blown out of the closet" (forced to come out) by their family because of the physical changes caused by HRT. After being blown out of the closet, nearly 70% of parents respond with doubt and blame; some resort to restricting freedom (37.4%) or physical/verbal violence (34.1%).

![家庭压力与个体困境](/pic/report/kiraequal-china-transgender-status-report-04.en.png)

**Analysis**: traditional values (such as carrying on the family line and fixed gender roles) are the main driver of family rejection. Because social support systems are absent, transgender minors depend heavily on their families financially and in everyday life (over half are highly dependent), leaving them almost powerless against domestic violence or being sent to "conversion therapy" institutions.
**Impact**: coming out remains dangerous, and family rejection directly leads to severe depression. As one respondent, Zijia, put it: "My family wants me to suppress my gender incongruence, marry, and have children — then the whole family would be happy." [international survey / 2019].
**Summary**: this is not an isolated pattern. When family love is deeply tied to conformity with one's assigned sex, many young people must make a cruel choice between "surviving by disguise" and "losing their family".

#### 5. Education and employment

**Problem**: invisible institutional discrimination keeps interrupting transgender people's academic and career paths.
**Evidence**: [Beijing Tongyu Culture / 2021] shows 70.8% of transgender students have been bullied on campus. By 2025, [MtF.Report / 2025] reveals an alarming education dropout rate: 47.7% of respondents have taken a break from or left school. On employment, transgender people face about three times the average risk of unemployment; because the law does not include "gender identity" in anti-discrimination, the few lawsuits that attracted attention were still hard to win [briefing / 2025].

**Analysis**: campuses lack gender-diversity education and anti-bullying interventions, leaving transgender students in a persistently hostile environment. And because gender markers on academic credentials cannot easily be changed, they face a first insurmountable hurdle on entering the workplace.
**Impact**: interrupted schooling and failed job hunting push a considerable share of transgender people into freelancing, live streaming, or the grey economy, leaving them in long-term economic instability and social marginalization.
**Summary**: society's inclusiveness can be measured by whether it allows a minority to take a mediocre but stable secular path — and today, that path remains strewn with thorns for transgender people.

#### 6. Community and public space

**Problem**: the transgender community depends heavily on virtual spaces, while public discussion space is tightening.
**Evidence**: [MtF.Report / 2025] notes that 78.5% of respondents say their city has "almost no" physical support resources. Community mutual aid has therefore moved largely online (QQ and WeChat groups). At the same time, [briefing / 2025] mentions that in recent years platform content moderation has tightened: some educational videos have been taken down, informational posts deleted, and accounts throttled or banned.

*
**Analysis**: without formal healthcare channels or offline social organizations, the internet becomes the only hub for resources and the one emotional sounding board. Yet intervention by internet censorship leaves the fragile information network at constant risk of breaking.
**Impact**: this not only cuts off channels for newcomers to get medical advice, but also deepens the community's isolation.
**Summary**: huddling together for warmth in virtual space is the community's instinct for self-protection; but mutual support behind a screen cannot fully shield against the cold of the real world.

#### 7. Media and public opinion

**Problem**: public attitudes have improved, but stigma and stereotypes remain deeply entrenched.
**Evidence**: on one hand, social media gives visibility to transgender influencers such as Abbily; on the other, controversy over "transgender people using public restrooms" often escalates into cyberbullying and demonization of the whole group [briefing / 2025]. In [MtF.Report / 2025], nearly 90% of respondents believe mainstream media present transgender women with stigmatizing or negative framing.
**Analysis**: the public often understands transgender people at the level of curiosity, or projects moral panic onto them. Public opinion shows a "distance gap": people can accept a trans celebrity on screen, but not the ordinary transgender person next door.
**Impact**: such prejudice seeps directly into daily life — more than half of respondents feel "fairly uncomfortable" or "very uncomfortable" in public settings (gatherings, clubs) [MtF.Report / 2025].

#### 8. 2020–2025 environmental trends

From the 2021 Beijing Tongyu Culture report to the 2025 broad survey, several trends stand out:

- Early awareness, late intervention: the age at which teenagers recognize their identity keeps falling, but medical policy (such as the 2022 specification) has not loosened, so illegal hormone access (77.1%) remains high.

- Overt family conflicts: as more people try HRT during adolescence, "passive blowing out of the closet" caused by bodily changes has become a new normal in family conflict.

- Atomized community support: as offline support shrinks under scrutiny, transgender people increasingly rely on person-to-person online mutual aid.

#### 9. Individual experiences and cases

Some respondents describe their situations like this:
Shanshan, 21, could barely leave her home because of severe anxiety and at one point considered suicide; her two cats are her deepest emotional anchor. She depends on hormones bought online. She says: "Wherever I go, I have to prepare enough hormones… stopping hormone therapy is immense torture." [Amnesty / 2019]
Another non-binary transgender person, Ran*, self-administered testosterone to make their voice lower. Facing confusion from others about their gender, they say: "I'm someone who has found a label that belongs to me. I define my own identity. No one defines me but me." [Amnesty / 2019]
These slices of lived experience remind us that a single clause in a policy document, or one standard in medical guidelines, becomes real pain or real relief in someone's long years.

### IV. Data Limitations

This report follows the following limitations:

- Sampling bias: [MtF.Report / 2025] relies on online distribution, so the sample may skew toward young trans women (MtF) who are comfortable with the internet; coverage of trans men (FtM), middle-aged and older people, and hidden populations in less developed regions may be insufficient.

- Conflicting data: estimates of the transgender population in China range widely (from 400,000 to 4 million), reflecting differences in definitions and statistical methods; this report does not force a single figure.

### V. Conclusions and Recommendations

**Conclusion**:
In 2025, transgender people in China are still in a "visible but unrecognized" squeeze. Although individuals awaken to their identities earlier and earlier, institutional barriers (medical restrictions centered on "parental consent"), unprotected campuses and workplaces, and an unstable online mutual-aid space together form a tight net. The vast majority of respondents struggle with persistent emotional distress, yet almost all express a strong desire for change.

**Recommendations**:

- Uncouple medical policy: the health authorities should update the Technical Management Specification for Gender Reassignment Technology, abolish unreasonable requirements for adults seeking gender-confirming care (such as forced parental consent, marriage status, etc.), and include HRT in standard clinical guidelines.

- Campus and family intervention: incorporate gender diversity awareness into education and campus anti-bullying mechanisms; provide social shelter channels for transgender minors in violent homes, and strictly crack down on forced "conversion therapy".

- Eliminate invisible discrimination: explicitly prohibit discrimination based on gender identity and gender expression in laws such as the Labor Law; simplify the process for changing gender markers on identity and academic documents.
